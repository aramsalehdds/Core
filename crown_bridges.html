Introduction to Fixed Prosthodontics

01
What is Fixed Prosthodontics?
DEFINITION

Fixed Prosthodontics is the branch of dentistry concerned with the replacement and restoration of teeth using restorations that are cemented or otherwise permanently attached — they cannot be removed by the patient.

Classification of Fixed Restorations
Inlay
Restoration that fits within a cavity preparation — does not cover cusps. Restored only the box/pit area. Intracoronal
Onlay
Covers one or more cusps. Provides cusp protection. Often indicated after large caries or endodontic treatment. Extracoronal
Veneer
Thin facing covering only the facial surface of a tooth. Ceramic or composite. Primarily esthetic. Partial coverage
¾ Crown
Covers lingual, proximal, and occlusal surfaces — facial surface left intact. Needs good alignment and esthetics. Partial coverage
Full Crown
Covers all surfaces of the clinical crown. All-metal, PFM, or all-ceramic. Most common extracoronal restoration. Full coverage
Fixed Partial Denture (FPD)
Replaces one or more missing teeth. Consists of retainer(s) + pontic(s) + connector(s). Commonly called a "bridge." Multi-unit

Indications for Crowns
INDICATE A CROWN WHEN:
  • Large caries undermining or approaching cusps (cusp protection needed)
  • Fractured tooth — fracture line compromising integrity
  • Endodontically treated posterior tooth (large access opening → cusp fracture risk)
  • Abutment tooth for a fixed partial denture or removable partial denture (RPD)
  • Severe wear, attrition, erosion, or abrasion (loss of vertical dimension)
  • Malformed or malaligned teeth (for which restorative approach is chosen over ortho)
  • Hypoplastic or hypocalcified teeth requiring full coverage
  • Root-treated tooth: especially mandibular posteriors and teeth with flared canals
Contraindications
AVOID A CROWN WHEN:
  • Insufficient crown height — clinical crown <3mm: retention will be inadequate without crown lengthening
  • Inadequate periodontal support — bone loss >50%, furcation involvement, mobility
  • Very young patients with large pulp chambers → risk of pulp exposure
  • Active caries or poor oral hygiene — must stabilise disease first
  • No clinical necessity — tooth restorable with a simpler restoration
  • Uncontrolled systemic disease or patient unable to tolerate treatment
Treatment Planning Sequence
SHILLINGBURG'S SEQUENCE — ALWAYS FOLLOW THIS ORDER
  1. Emergency treatment — relieve pain, acute infection
  2. Disease control — caries control, oral hygiene instruction, scaling
  3. Periodontal treatment — re-evaluate after 6-8 weeks
  4. Endodontic treatment — root canals before crown preparation
  5. Surgical treatment — crown lengthening, extractions, implant placement
  6. Orthodontic treatment (if planned)
  7. Prosthetic treatment — start posterior, then anterior
  8. Maintenance/recall — 3–6 monthly intervals initially

Principles of Crown Preparation
The five foundational principles governing all crown preparations, retention and resistance form, margin types, biologic width, and preparation taper.
Shillingburg Ch. 6–8 Rosenstiel Ch. 7–8
02
Shillingburg's 5 Principles of Crown Preparation
1

Preservation of Tooth Structure

Remove only what is necessary. Minimum necessary reduction to achieve the clinical goal — no more. Excessive removal weakens the tooth, risks pulp exposure, and reduces the long-term prognosis of the tooth.

2

Retention and Resistance Form

Retention prevents removal along the path of insertion. Resistance prevents displacement by oblique or lateral forces. Achieved primarily through taper, height, and surface area of preparation walls.

3

Structural Durability

The restoration must have adequate bulk to withstand occlusal forces without fracture or deformation. Different materials require different minimum thicknesses.

4

Marginal Integrity

A precise, well-defined, continuous finish line allows the lab to create a well-fitting restoration margin. Poor margins = microleakage, secondary caries, periodontal disease.

5

Preservation of Periodontium

Respect biologic width. Supragingival margins preferred. If subgingival, must remain ≥2mm from alveolar bone crest. Crown contours must promote cleansability.

Total Occlusal Convergence (TOC) / Taper
KEY CONCEPT — TAPER

TOC is the angle formed between two opposing prepared axial walls. Ideal = 6° TOC (3° per wall). This provides optimal retention while allowing the crown to be seated. Clinically, 10–20° is considered acceptable.

Biologic Width — Critical Concept
VIOLATION = GINGIVAL INFLAMMATION + BONE LOSS

Biologic width is the dimension of soft tissue attachment to the tooth above the alveolar bone. First described by Gargiulo et al. (1961).

  • Epithelial attachment: ~0.69mm
  • Connective tissue attachment: ~1.07mm
  • Total biologic width: ~2.04mm
  • Sulcus depth: ~0.69mm
  • Total from bone to gingival margin: ~3mm

Retention & Resistance — Factors That Affect Them
↑ Increases Retention
  • Less taper (closer to 0°)
  • Greater crown height/length
  • Greater diameter
  • Rougher internal surface
  • More retentive cement
  • Grooves, boxes, pins (anti-rotation)
↓ Decreases Retention
  • Greater taper (>20°)
  • Short clinical crown (<3mm)
  • Small diameter (mandibular incisors)
  • Over-prepared, smooth walls
  • Weak temporary cement used long-term
  • Wet contamination at cementation
SHILLINGBURG ON ANTI-ROTATION

For short or conical preparations that lack adequate height, proximal grooves must be added to provide anti-rotation and resistance to lateral displacement. Without them, the crown can lever off under non-axial forces. This is especially important for premolars and conical teeth.

General Preparation Protocol — Clinical Steps
1

Shade Selection

Take shade before preparation — tooth dehydrates during drilling and becomes lighter. Use natural or daylight-corrected light. Wet the tooth. Record shade in chart.

2

Local Anaesthesia

Adequate anaesthesia. For subgingival margin — also infiltrate buccally for haemostasis (with vasoconstrictor).

3

Occlusal/Incisal Reduction — Depth Cuts First

Use a depth-cut bur to create guide grooves (1.5mm posterior functional cusp, 2.0mm for all-ceramic). Then connect them. Do not freehand — depth cuts prevent over- or under-preparation.

4

Axial Wall Reduction

Reduce buccal and lingual walls. Maintain 6° taper (3° per wall). Ensure adequate thickness for restorative material. Use end-cutting bur or tapered diamond.

5

Proximal Reduction

Use flame-shaped bur. Break contact with adjacent tooth. Keep bur parallel to path of insertion. Avoid damage to adjacent teeth — use matrix band if necessary.

6

Margin Preparation (Finish Line)

Establish correct margin type for material. Use appropriate bur: round-end tapered for chamfer; flat-end tapered for shoulder. Margin must be distinct, continuous, and smooth.

7

Finishing and Smoothing

Use fine/extra-fine diamonds or stones. Remove all sharp line angles. Smooth all surfaces. Sharp angles concentrate stress and make wax-up/fabrication harder.

8

Evaluation

Check: taper with parallel probe, occlusal clearance (articulating paper or wax), margin continuity with explorer, surface quality. Take pre-impression radiograph if subgingival margin.

Bur Selection Guide
🔴 Depth-Cut Bur (Torpedo / Wheel)

Creates depth grooves on occlusal surface to guide reduction. Available in 1.5mm and 2.0mm depths. Always use depth cuts first — prevents over or under reduction.

🔷 Flat-End Tapered Diamond

Used for occlusal reduction and shoulder preparation (90° finish line). Also for axial reduction when shoulder margin required.

🔶 Round-End Tapered Diamond

For chamfer finish lines on axial walls. The rounded tip creates the concave chamfer profile. Most versatile bur in crown prep.

🔥 Flame-Shaped Diamond

For proximal reduction and reaching into tight contacts. Also used for interproximal finishing without damaging adjacent teeth.

✂️ Fine-Grit Diamond

Finishing the preparation. Smooth all surfaces, refine margin. Reduces micro-cracks in preparation walls that could weaken the tooth.

🗡️ Carbide Finishing Bur

Final smoothing of preparation. Removes diamond grit scratches. Creates very smooth surface for optimal impression detail.

PFM Crown Preparation — Step by Step
PREPARATION ORDER — PFM (POSTERIOR TOOTH)

Always follow this sequence for consistency and to prevent accidental over-reduction.

1

Occlusal Reduction — Depth Cuts

Place depth-cut grooves: 1.5mm (functional cusp side) and 1.5mm (non-functional) on occlusal surface. Then connect and reduce entire occlusal by grinding between grooves. Check clearance with wax wafer or shimstock in occlusion.

2

Buccal Reduction — Depth Cuts First

Score three vertical depth grooves on buccal surface 1.5mm deep. Reduce between grooves. Two planes: follow occlusal 2/3 and gingival 1/3 separately to maintain tooth form.

3

Lingual Reduction

Reduce lingual surface 1.0–1.5mm. For lower posterior: buccal reduction is the porcelain-coverage side, lingual can be metal only. Maintain taper throughout.

4

Proximal Reduction

Use flame-shaped bur to break proximal contact. Keep bur parallel to path of insertion. Avoid trauma to adjacent teeth — use thin matrix band to protect them. Ensure adequate interproximal clearance for the lab.

5

Facial Margin — Shoulder Preparation

Prepare 1.0–1.5mm shoulder on facial aspect. Use flat-end tapered bur. Margin should be placed 0.5–1.0mm subgingival for esthetics. Ensure shoulder is at right angle to long axis of tooth.

6

Lingual Margin — Chamfer

Prepare 0.3–0.5mm chamfer on lingual and proximal margins (where metal will show). Use round-end tapered bur. Continuous with facial margin.

7

Finishing

Fine diamond then carbide bur to smooth all surfaces. Remove all sharp line angles and point angles. Sharp angles create stress concentration sites. Round all transitions between planes.

8

Final Evaluation

Check: 2mm occlusal clearance (wax wafer), continuous margin (explorer), adequate taper (paralleling instrument), no undercuts (block out or adjust). Take pre-impression periapical radiograph to verify subgingival margin if applicable.

Conservative vs Full Coverage — Decision Tree
CHOOSE PARTIAL COVERAGE WHEN:
  • Substantial sound tooth structure remains — especially on the facial surface
  • Excellent axial alignment (no tilt or rotation)
  • No esthetic requirement on facial surface (posterior teeth)
  • Young patient — large pulp chamber makes full prep risky
  • Patient values tooth conservation

  • Multiple surfaces involved with caries or old restorations
  • Tooth tilted or rotated (needs path of insertion adjustment)
  • Abutment for FPD with significant forces
  • Heavily restored with inadequate remaining tooth structure
  • Post-endodontic treatment (large pulp access + weakened structure)

Inlay vs Onlay — The Critical Difference
⚠️ Inlay — Cusp Weakening Risk

An inlay sits within the cavity. The remaining cusps are unsupported by the restoration. Under occlusal loading, the cusps can flex and fracture. Risk increases with wider cavities (>1/3 intercuspal width).

Rule: If the cavity width exceeds 1/3 of the intercuspal distance → convert to an onlay for cusp protection.

✓ Onlay — Cusp Protection

An onlay covers the cusp(s), binding them together and protecting them from flexural fracture. Especially critical for endodontically treated posterior teeth which lose tooth structure resilience (Sedgley & Messer, 1992 — ETT teeth are more brittle).

Also called "overlay" or "MOD onlay" — cusp-capping is its defining feature.

¾ Crown — Posterior
Preparation Features
  • Occlusal reduction: 1.0–1.5mm (same as full crown for the covered area)
  • Facial cusp bevel: 30–45° bevel on buccal cusp tip — creates a chamfer that lies within the enamel and is out of the esthetic zone
  • Lingual wall: Reduce 1.0mm with chamfer margin
  • Proximal grooves: MANDATORY — provide resistance form against rotational displacement (since facial wall is absent). Placed at the junction of the buccal line angle, parallel to path of insertion.
  • Margin: Chamfer or shoulder at lingual; ends just facial to proximal embrasure (stays out of esthetic zone)
MOD Onlay — When & How
INDICATIONS FOR MOD ONLAY
  • Existing large MOD amalgam / composite needing replacement
  • Cavity width >1/3 intercuspal distance
  • Endodontically treated posterior tooth (cusp fracture prevention)
  • Cracked tooth syndrome (splint the cusps)
  • When cusp replacement is needed in addition to proximal restoration
Onlay Preparation Features
  • Occlusal isthmus: Depth = 1.5–2.0mm (matches crown reduction for the occlusal area)
  • Cusp reduction: 1.0–1.5mm for gold; 2.0mm for ceramic
  • Buccal cusp bevel: 45° bevel creates "cusp cap" — removes brittle enamel margins, increases area of contact
  • Proximal boxes: 90° walls, clear of contact area, axiopulpal line angle rounded
  • Reverse bevel: On buccal and lingual walls to create positive seat
  • No undercuts: All walls must have a single path of insertion
"The onlay has a distinct advantage over the inlay: it protects the remaining cusps from fracture by covering them and binding them to the restoration, rather than leaving them as unsupported enamel columns."
— Shillingburg, Fundamentals of Fixed Prosthodontics, 4th ed.
MOD Onlay Preparation — Clinical Steps
1

Outline Form

Remove existing restoration. Extend preparation to clear caries margins. All walls diverge slightly occlusally (path of insertion is occlusal).

2

Occlusal Isthmus

Create isthmus depth of 1.5–2.0mm. Width: just enough to clear the old restoration and any caries. Axiopulpal line angle must be rounded to prevent stress concentration.

3

Proximal Boxes

Extend proximally to clear contact areas. Gingival floor: flat, 90° to long axis. Buccal and lingual walls: diverge slightly toward occlusal. Ensure buccal and lingual walls clear embrasures for hygienic access.

4

Cusp Reduction

Reduce all cusp tips by 1.5mm (gold) or 2.0mm (ceramic). Follow the cusp form — do not flatten cusps. Preserve functional cusp form.

5

Cusp Bevel / Cap

Place 45° bevel on buccal and lingual cusp slopes. This bevel creates a positive seat, removes thin enamel margins, and increases casting bulk at margins.

6

Finishing

Smooth all line angles. Ensure no undercuts. Check path of insertion with probe or mirror. Place provisional restoration (composite or direct acrylic) and check occlusion.

Ante's Law
ANTE'S LAW (1926)

"The combined perimeter of the roots of all abutment teeth should equal or exceed that of the teeth to be replaced."

Meaning: the periodontal support (root surface area) of the abutment teeth must be at least equal to the periodontal support of the pontic(s) they're replacing. This is a guideline, not an absolute rule.

Clinical Application of Ante's Law
  • Replacing one premolar with two molar abutments → usually fine (molar roots > premolar)
  • Long-span bridge replacing 3–4 teeth from two premolars → questionable — need to assess bone support
  • Violated Ante's law → abutments may be overloaded → mobile abutments → bridge failure
  • Modern perspective: crown-root ratio and bone quality are equally important
Connector Design
Rigid Connector

Cast in one piece or soldered. No movement between units. Most common type. Used for most FPDs. The connector must be 4mm² minimum area (mesiodistal × faciolingual).

Non-Rigid Connector (Key & Keyway)

Allows slight movement. Use for: Long-span bridges, pier abutments (tooth in middle of bridge), and when abutment teeth have different paths of insertion. The "key" is a dove-tail shaped male attachment; the "keyway" is the female socket.

PIER ABUTMENT — NON-RIGID CONNECTOR IS MANDATORY

When there's a tooth in the middle of a 5-unit bridge (e.g., molar–premolar–missing–incisor–missing), the middle tooth (pier abutment) acts as a fulcrum. This dislodges one end of the bridge. Solution: place a non-rigid connector distal to the pier abutment to relieve this stress.

1

1

Select Impression Tray

Gingival Retraction

Remove Top Cord, Dry & Rinse

Snap out with firm, quick motion (avoid rocking). Inspect for: voids at margin, tears, air bubbles, incomplete seating. Discard and retake if margin is not captured perfectly.

Pour immediately or within manufacturer's time window. PVS: can store up to 2 weeks (check manufacturer). Polyether: pour within 1 hour. Use Type IV dental stone for working die.

Dimensions of Tooth Colour
Value (Brightness)

The most important dimension. Lightness vs darkness of colour. Ranges from white to black independent of hue. High value = bright/light. Low value = dark. Match value first — errors here are most noticeable.

Chroma (Saturation)

The intensity or concentration of colour. High chroma = vivid, saturated colour. Low chroma = pale, washed-out. Cervical third of teeth typically highest chroma.

Hue (Colour)

The basic colour family — yellow, orange, reddish-brown, etc. Human teeth are in the yellow-orange range. Least important perceptually of the three dimensions.

Metamerism — Critical Concept
METAMERISM

Two objects may match in colour under one type of light but appear different colours under a different light source. This occurs because different light sources have different spectral distributions.

Clinical significance: A crown that looks perfect in the dental office may look mismatched in daylight (outdoor, natural light) or in the patient's office (fluorescent lighting). Always check shade in multiple lighting conditions.

Digital Shade Matching
Spectrophotometers (e.g., VITA Easyshade)
  • Measures the spectral reflectance of the tooth
  • Converts to L*a*b* colour space (or L*C*h*)
  • More objective and reproducible than visual shade-taking
  • Not affected by: clinician's colour perception, fatigue, lighting
  • Limitations: affected by surface texture, translucency; can't replace artistic assessment entirely
  • Results can be directly communicated to the lab digitally
Shade Selection Protocol — Step by Step
1

Timing

Take shade BEFORE preparation begins. Preparation dehydrates the tooth → it becomes whiter (temporarily). If shade taken after prep, it will be wrongly selected too light.

2

Lighting

Use natural daylight or a daylight-simulating lamp. North-facing window is ideal. Avoid: direct sunlight, fluorescent office lights, yellow tungsten lights. Check under multiple lights (metamerism).

3

Patient Preparation

Remove colourful clothing and jewellery (interfere with colour perception). Gray or neutral background is ideal. Ask patient not to wear bright lipstick.

4

Wet the Tooth

Wet the tooth and shade tab with water. Dry surfaces scatter light differently and falsely appear lighter.

5

DIE SPACER — WHAT IT DOES

Die spacer creates a cement space (25–50 microns) between the crown and the die wall — but NOT at the margin (stops 1mm short). Without this space, the cement cannot flow out during seating and the crown won't seat fully. Stopping short of the margin ensures a perfect marginal seal.

Marginal Fit — Acceptable Limits
Marginal Gap Standards
  • McLean & von Fraunhofer (1971): clinically acceptable marginal gap = <120 microns
  • Ideal: <50 microns (achievable with high-quality lab work)
  • Assess with: explorer tip, digital palpation, periapical radiograph (proximal margins), Fit Checker (silicone-based internal fit indicator)
  • No rocking — rocking indicates unequal seating or a high spot internally
Proximal Contact Assessment
Correct Contact

Dental floss passes through with a slight "snap" — feels snug but not impossible. If floss shreds or cannot pass → contact too tight. If floss falls through freely → no contact (open contact = food trap).

Shimstock (Metal Foil) Test

8-micron shimstock held between teeth while patient closes. A well-seated crown should hold the shimstock on the contact side. Tests for centric contacts and contact tightness with extreme precision.

Try-in Clinical Protocol
1

Remove Provisional

Remove provisional carefully. Clean the preparation with pumice and brush. Remove all temporary cement — residue interferes with seating. Rinse and dry.

2

Seat the Crown

Seat without force initially. Check if it fully seats. If resistance: check contacts first (most common), then internal fit with Fit Checker.

3

Check Marginal Fit

Run explorer around entire circumference. Should feel smooth transition from crown to tooth. No step, ledge, or detectable gap. Take periapical X-ray of proximal margins.

4

Check Proximal Contacts

Dental floss: mesial and distal. Should snap through with resistance. Adjust tight contacts with thin metal strips or fine bur.

5

Check Occlusion

Articulating paper (200μm red, then 40μm blue). First check centric occlusion — even marks on crown and all other posterior teeth. Then check lateral excursions — no interfering contacts on crown if canine guidance or group function is planned.

6

Check Esthetics

Patient mirror. Check shade in natural light. Assess shape, contour, embrasure form. Patient approval — document in notes.

7

Decision: Cement or Return

If all criteria satisfied → proceed to cementation. If any major issue (shade, margin, contacts not correctable chairside) → return to lab with documented corrections.

Zinc Phosphate — The Classic
Mixing Technique (Cold Slab Method)
  • Use a cold (refrigerated or chilled) glass slab to slow exothermic reaction
  • Spatulate over a large area of the slab (increases working time)
  • Incremental powder addition — add powder in small amounts to liquid
  • Correct consistency: thin enough to flow into margins, forms string 25mm before breaking
  • Working time: ~5 minutes; set time: ~7–9 minutes
  • Initial pH: 3.5 (acidic) → rises to neutral over minutes
Resin Cement — Adhesive Protocol
Conventional Resin (e.g., Variolink, Panavia)
  • Tooth surface: Etch with 37% phosphoric acid (15–30 sec enamel, 15 sec dentin) → rinse → bond agent → light-cure
  • Restoration surface: All-ceramic → clean → etch with HF acid (ceramic etchant) → silanate → bond agent
  • Dual-cure resin cement: light-cure accessible areas, chemical-cure does the rest
  • Remove excess cement while "gel" stage before full cure for easy removal
Cementation Clinical Protocol
1

Final Try-in & Occlusal Check

One final check of fit, contacts and occlusion before committing to permanent cementation.

2

Clean the Preparation

Remove temporary cement with scaler and ultrasonic. Pump the preparation with pumice. Rinse thoroughly. Clean the crown internally with alcohol or specific cleaner. No saliva contact after cleaning.

3

Isolate

Cotton rolls and saliva ejector (minimum). Rubber dam is gold standard for resin cementation. Contamination at this stage is the most common cause of early cement failure.

4

Prepare Tooth & Crown Surfaces (if Resin Cement)

Tooth: etch, prime, bond. Crown: HF etch (ceramic), silane coupling agent, bond agent. Allow all adhesive steps correct time and cure.

5

Mix and Apply Cement

Mix cement. Load ~1/3 of internal surface of crown. Seat with finger pressure. Patient bites on orangewood stick for additional seating pressure. Verify fully seated.

6

Remove Excess Cement

Zinc phosphate/GI: allow to partially set, remove in chunks. Resin: remove gel-stage excess with explorer and floss before light-curing inaccessible areas. Check all subgingival areas — retained cement causes peri-implantitis/periodontitis.

7

Check Occlusion Again

Final occlusal check after cementation. Cement film increases vertical dimension slightly — adjust any prematurities. Check all excursions.

8

Patient Instructions

Avoid sticky/hard foods for 24 hours (cement continues to harden). Maintain good oral hygiene. Report any sensitivity lasting >2 weeks. Schedule recall.

Secondary Caries — The Most Common Cause of Crown Failure
MOST COMMON CAUSE OF CROWN FAILURE

Secondary (recurrent) caries accounts for the majority of crown replacements. Risk factors: poor marginal fit (>120μm), inadequate patient oral hygiene, cariogenic diet, xerostomia, poor marginal seal of cement. Prevention: regular recall, GIC or RMGI cements (fluoride release), patient education.

De-cementation — Why Crowns Come Loose
Causes of De-cementation
  • Inadequate preparation retention (over-tapered, too short)
  • Cement dissolution over time (zinc phosphate is soluble)
  • Occlusal overload / parafunctional habits
  • Secondary caries under crown → cement failure
  • Contamination at time of cementation (moisture)
  • Wrong cement for material (e.g., ZnPO4 for all-ceramic)
Management
  • Assess crown internally and tooth preparation for caries
  • If no caries, intact crown, intact tooth → re-cement
  • If inadequate retention → address root cause (remake with better preparation or add features)
  • Clean both surfaces thoroughly before re-cementation
  • Choose more retentive cement if de-cemented before
Managing a Loose Crown — Clinical Protocol
1

Assess the Situation

Is the crown intact? Is the tooth intact? Any caries? Radiograph the tooth (look for periapical changes, caries at margin). Identify why it failed before re-cementing.

2

Clean Crown and Preparation

Remove all old cement from inside the crown (ultrasonic, scaler, bur). Remove old cement from tooth surface. Polish preparation surface with pumice.

3

Address Root Cause

If caries: restore before re-cementation. If poor retention: consider adding grooves/pins, or remaking. If bruxism: provide night guard after re-cementation.

4

Re-cement

Select appropriate cement. If first cement was zinc phosphate and crown is repeatedly loose → upgrade to RMGI or resin cement. Follow standard cementation protocol.

Managing Porcelain Fracture
1

Assess Severity

Small superficial chip (non-functional area): polish with fine diamond and rubber wheel. Medium fracture: intraoral porcelain repair kit. Large fracture in functional area: remake.

2

Intraoral Porcelain Repair

Etch fractured surface with HF etchant (or phosphoric acid as alternative). Apply silane coupling agent. Bond + composite repair material. Finish and polish.

3

Assess Occlusion & Parafunction

Identify if bruxism or heavy occlusal contacts caused the fracture. Fabricate night guard / occlusal splint. Adjust occlusion to redistribute forces before remaking.
