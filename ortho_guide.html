Direction of Facial Growth
KEY PRINCIPLE

The face grows predominantly downward and forward. The nasomaxillary complex is carried downward and forward as it grows. The mandible grows by condylar growth — also downward and forward, but the net direction depends on the balance between vertical and horizontal components.

  • Primary site: condylar cartilage (secondary cartilage)

Growth Peaks — Sex Differences
Girls 👧
  • Peak growth spurt: 11–12 years
  • Growth complete: ~16–17 years
  • CS3 occurs earlier than boys
  • Functional appliance window closes earlier
Boys 👦
  • Peak growth spurt: 13–14 years
  • Growth complete: ~18–20 years (mandible especially)
  • CS3 occurs later than girls
  • Longer window for growth modification
CLASS III WARNING — Late Mandibular Growth

Boys with Class III malocclusion can continue mandibular growth well into their late teens and early 20s. This is why orthognathic surgery for Class III is deferred until at least 18yr in females and 20yr in males — to ensure growth is truly complete before surgery.

Overjet vs Overbite
Overjet (OJ)

Horizontal distance from the labial surface of the upper central incisor to the labial surface of the lower central incisor, measured parallel to the occlusal plane. Normal: 2–4mm. Measured at the upper incisor (not the lower).

Overbite (OB)

Vertical overlap of the upper incisors over the lower incisors. Normal: 2–4mm (about 1/3 of lower incisor crown height). Complete overbite = lower incisors contact palatal mucosa or upper teeth. Traumatic = tissue damage occurs.

OJ vs OB MEMORY TIP

OJ = Jet (horizontal, forward motion) — measured horizontally, like a jet flying forward. OB = Bite (vertical) — how much one bites over the other vertically. OJ measured at upper incisor. OB measured as vertical overlap.

Interpreting Cephalometrics — Clinical Framework
1

Skeletal Pattern — Sagittal (ANB)

ANB >4° = Class II skeletal | 2–4° = Class I | <2° or negative = Class III. Remember ANB is affected by rotation of the jaws — a high MMPA increases ANB artifactually.

2

Vertical Pattern (MMPA)

<23° = hypodivergent (deep bite tendency, anterior rotation). 23–31° = average. >31° = hyperdivergent (open bite tendency, posterior rotation). Critical for treatment planning — hyperivergent patients cannot have headgear causing molar extrusion.

3

Dental — Upper Incisor (UI/Max)

Normal = 108°. In Class II/1: UI/Max usually elevated (>112° proclined). In Class II/2: UI/Max reduced (<103° retroclined). Treatment planning must include normalising incisor position.

4

Dental — Lower Incisor (LI/Mand)

Lower incisor position is critical for stability. Moving lower incisors excessively forward has poor long-term stability. The lower incisor should sit at or near its original position after treatment (or within the "zone of stability").

5

Soft Tissue Assessment

E-line (Ricketts): tip of nose to soft tissue Pg. Upper lip: 4mm behind | Lower lip: 2mm behind. Nasolabial angle (normal: 90–110°). Profile: convex (Class II), straight (Class I), concave (Class III).

WITS APPRAISAL — When ANB is Unreliable

Wits measures the distance between points AO and BO (perpendicular projections of A and B onto the occlusal plane). Less affected by jaw rotation artefact than ANB. Normal: girls = 0mm; boys = -1mm. Positive Wits = Class II tendency. Negative Wits = Class III tendency.

Arch Length Discrepancy (ALD)
DEFINITION

ALD = Available arch length (space in the arch from 6-6, measured along arch perimeter) minus Required arch length (sum of mesiodistal widths of all teeth from 6-6). A negative ALD = crowding. Positive ALD = spacing.

Bolton Analysis — Tooth Size Discrepancy
Bolton Ratios (1958)
  • Anterior ratio: (Sum of 6 lower widths / Sum of 6 upper widths) × 100 = 77.2% ± 1.65%
  • Overall ratio: (Sum of 12 lower widths / Sum of 12 upper widths) × 100 = 91.3% ± 1.91%
  • If >77.2%: excess lower tooth material → spacing in upper or crowding in lower at end of treatment
  • If <77.2%: excess upper tooth material → spacing in lower arch or spaces in upper
  • Relevant when: Cases with spaced upper arch and crowded lower, or lateral incisor anomalies (peg laterals)
Mixed Dentition Space Analysis
Moyers Analysis

Uses the width of the mandibular permanent incisors to predict the widths of unerupted canines and premolars. Uses probability tables at the 75th percentile (most clinically useful).

Tanaka-Johnston Method

Simple formula. For each arch: measure width of 4 lower incisors, divide by 2, add 10.5 (upper) or 10.5 (lower)... Actually: lower C+P1+P2 = (sum of 4 LI / 2) + 10.5mm | upper = same + 11.0mm

  • Tooth to be extracted: good restorability prognosis? (don't extract sound premolars if a compromised molar is the real problem)
  • Asymmetric extractions: only for asymmetric crowding or midline correction — plan carefully

MOCDO — Priority Recording Order for DHC

When assessing IOTN, record the WORST feature using the MOCDO priority:

  • Missing teeth (hypodontia, supernumeraries)
  • Overjet (increased or reverse)
  • Crossbite (anterior or posterior)
  • Displacements (crowding)
  • Overbite (increased, reduced, open bite)

Grade the WORST single feature you find. The grade of the whole case = that worst feature's grade.

Aesthetic Component (AC)
AC Grades 1–10

The AC uses 10 photographs of increasing dental attractiveness. The patient's dentition is compared to these reference photographs. Grade 1 = most attractive. Grade 10 = least attractive.

  • AC 1–4: Dental appearance acceptable
  • AC 5–7: Borderline — moderate treatment need
  • AC 8–10: Definitely unacceptable appearance — definite treatment need
DHC Grade 4 Features — Know These in Detail
Grade 4a — Increased OJ

OJ >6mm but ≤9mm. This means the upper incisors are 6–9mm in front of lowers — significant risk of trauma, lip incompetence, and psychosocial impact.

Grade 4b — Reverse OJ

Reverse OJ of 1–3.5mm. Class III relationship causing functional concerns, potential for anterior displacement of mandible.

Grade 4c — Crossbite discrepancy

Crossbite with >2mm discrepancy between RCP and ICP. This is a significant displacement causing asymmetric wear and TMJ loading.

Grade 4f — Crowding

Contact point displacement >4mm. Significant crowding, impaction risk, hygiene difficulty, and aesthetic concern.

ICON — Index of Complexity, Outcome, Need
ICON (Daniels & Richmond, 2000)

More comprehensive than IOTN. Assesses: AC (weighted ×7), upper arch crowding/spacing, crossbite, OB (anterior vertical), buccal segment anteroposterior relationship. Used in some practices as a treatment difficulty index as well as a need index.

Archwire Sequence — Treatment Phases
1

Alignment (Levelling)

Light NiTi (nickel-titanium) wires. 0.014" round NiTi → 0.018" round NiTi. Flexible, light continuous force. Levels and aligns teeth. Avoids heavy forces that cause root resorption.

2

Working Wires

Stiffer wires: 0.018" NiTi or 0.019×0.025" NiTi. More control. Space closure with power chain or springs. Some torque expression begins.

3

Finishing

Rectangular stainless steel: 0.019×0.025" SS. Maximum torque expression. Final detailing. Coordination of arches. Preparation for debond or retention.

Headgear Types
Cervical Pull

Force directed downward and backward. Extrudes and retracts upper molars. Used in patients with normal or reduced lower face height. Risk: increases vertical dimension — avoid in high angle patients.

High Pull

Force directed upward and backward. Intrudes and retracts upper molars. Better for high-angle (hyperdivergent) patients. Reduces vertical dimension.

Combination Pull

Force through the centre of resistance of upper molar. Retracts without vertical change. Most common for general Class II correction.

HEADGEAR SAFETY — MANDATORY WARNING

All patients wearing headgear MUST have a safety facebow (locking mechanism on inner bow to prevent laceration of eyes/face if headgear is pulled). This is a clinical governance requirement. Headgear injuries, though rare, have caused serious ocular trauma. Fully warn patients and fit locking facebow only.

What is Anchorage?
DEFINITION

Anchorage = resistance to unwanted tooth movement. By Newton's 3rd Law, every force applied to move a tooth generates an equal and opposite force on the anchor teeth. Anchorage planning determines which teeth will move (active) and which will resist moving (anchor).

Anchorage Requirements
Maximum Anchorage

Minimal or zero molar movement acceptable. Used when: extracting and needing to bring anterior teeth back without molars drifting forward. Clinical example: significant OJ reduction where upper incisors must retract fully into extraction space without any molar mesial drift.

Moderate Anchorage

Balanced: some molar drift mesially, some incisor retraction. Most common scenario. Space closed 50/50 between anterior retraction and posterior drift.

Minimum Anchorage

Molars allowed to drift maximally mesially. Used when: crowding is mainly in the posterior, and anterior teeth need to stay or move slightly forward. Example: spacing in anterior with molar drift needed to close it.

TADs — Temporary Anchorage Devices
Mini-screws (Orthodontic Implants)
  • Titanium or stainless steel screws placed into bone (1.2–2.0mm diameter, 6–12mm length)
  • Provide absolute anchorage — do not move regardless of force applied
  • Placed between roots under LA — minimally invasive
  • Indications: Maximum anchorage cases, intrusion of over-erupted teeth, tooth extrusion, Class II elastics without headgear, molar distalization
  • Success rate: ~80–90% (failure usually from mobility, infection, or patient factors)
  • Removed easily when no longer needed
Anchorage Reinforcement Methods
Palatal Arch (TPA / Nance Button)

Trans-palatal arch (TPA): wire connecting upper 6s across palate. Prevents molar rotation and tipping. Nance button: acrylic pad on TPA resting on palate — adds extra anchorage via tissue contact.

Lingual Arch (Lower)

Wire connecting lower 6s lingually. Prevents molar mesial drift and maintains arch width. Used for maximum anchorage in lower arch before Class II mechanics.

Headgear

Extra-oral anchorage. Force applied to upper molars directed backward (and up or down depending on type). Wears 14 hrs/day minimum for significant skeletal effect. Adds to anchorage AND corrects Class II.

Segment Arch Technique

Posterior teeth tied together as one rigid unit. Increases collective root surface area → more anchorage. Used in extraction cases requiring maximum anterior retraction.

Pressure-Tension Theory (Schwartz)
Pressure Side

PDL fibres compressed → blood vessels occluded → osteoclasts recruited → bone resorption. Light force: direct (frontal) resorption — osteoclasts from PDL side → faster movement. Heavy force: hyalinisation → indirect (undermining) resorption → slower movement.

Tension Side

PDL fibres stretched → blood vessels dilated → osteoblasts stimulated → bone deposition. New bone fills the space left by the moving tooth.

Hyalinization — The Problem with Heavy Forces
HYALINIZATION

With excessive force, the PDL is completely compressed → the tissue becomes necrotic and acellular (hyaline zone — looks like glass histologically). No osteoclasts can access this zone from the PDL side. Movement STOPS. Osteoclasts must tunnel in from the bone marrow (indirect/undermining resorption) → this is slower, less predictable, and more likely to cause root resorption. This is why lighter, continuous forces are better than heavy, intermittent forces.

Identify the Cause

Rapid Maxillary Expansion (RME)
Hyrax Appliance — Protocol
  • Indication: Bilateral posterior crossbite (true or displacement), narrow upper arch, before or during fixed appliance treatment
  • Age: Before midpalatal suture fuses — girls <16yr, boys <17yr (approximately)
  • Activation: 1 turn = 0.25mm expansion. Typical: 2 turns/day (0.5mm/day) for rapid expansion
  • Total expansion needed: overcorrect by 2–3mm (accounts for relapse)
  • Active phase: 2–3 weeks (until 2–3mm overcorrection achieved)
  • Retention phase: 3–6 months (allow bone to fill suture)
  • Diastema: Median diastema opens during RME — closes spontaneously in most cases after retention
  • Adults: SARPE (surgically assisted) if suture fused

  • Single upper incisor in crossbite

  • No skeletal component

  • Multiple teeth in crossbite
  • Skeletal Class III base (negative ANB)

This is one of the clinical justifications for early treatment of significant Class II/1 — reducing incisor trauma risk as well as psychosocial impact. IOTN DHC grade 4a = OJ >6mm (definite NHS treatment need).

Psychosocial Impact
Evidence Base

Class II/1 with significantly increased OJ has documented psychosocial impact on children — increased teasing/bullying, reduced self-confidence. This is acknowledged in IOTN and is a legitimate indication for treatment alongside health/functional reasons. Assessment should include patient-reported concerns.

Twin Block Appliance — Protocol & Results
Twin Block (Clark, 1982) — Key Facts
  • Design: Upper and lower acrylic blocks with 70° bite planes that engage each other, posturing the mandible forward (typically 5–7mm forward construction bite)
  • Wear: Full time — 24 hours including meals. This is critical for success — part-time wear gives dental not skeletal effects.
  • Active phase: 6–12 months typically
  • Success requires: Growing patient (CS2–3), good compliance, adequate freeway space for bite blocks
  • OJ reduction: ~6–7mm total: approx 3mm skeletal change (mandibular growth augmented) + 3–4mm dental change (upper incisors retroclined, lower incisors proclined)
  • After Twin Block: Fixed appliance for detailed alignment and occlusion
  • Clark Twin Block is the most evidence-based functional appliance for Class II treatment
TWIN BLOCK RESULTS — FROM BASS (1982) STUDIES

Average OJ reduction with Twin Block in growing patients: 6–7mm. Of this: ~50% dental, ~50% skeletal. The skeletal effect is genuine mandibular growth augmentation at the condyle. After growth cessation, some relapse of skeletal component occurs (10–15%). The dental effect is more stable if good occlusion is achieved at the end of fixed appliance phase.

Two-Phase vs One-Phase Treatment
CLINICAL CONTROVERSY — TREAT EARLY OR WAIT?

Studies (Tulloch, Proffit) show that two-phase treatment (functional at age 10–11 + fixed at 12–14) gives the SAME final outcome as one-phase fixed treatment at age 12–14. The advantage of early treatment is: psychosocial benefit, reduced trauma risk, and treating the patient closer to peak growth. The disadvantage: longer overall treatment time, more appointments, more retention period. Most UK specialists recommend treatment at CS2-3 for significant cases (OJ >7mm), and one-phase fixed treatment for milder cases.

Aetiology
The Tight Lower Lip Theory

The lower lip in Class II/2 rests above the upper central incisors (because OJ is small) and acts as a retruding force on the centrals. This is why they are retroclined. The lateral incisors escape this lip pressure and appear proclined. This is a soft tissue-driven dental development. It typically runs in families (strong genetic component).

Deep Overbite in Class II/2
TRAUMATIC OVERBITE

Deep overbite in Class II/2 can be traumatic — lower incisors traumatically contact the palatal mucosa, or upper incisors traumatically contact the labial gingiva of the lower incisors. This causes soft tissue trauma, recession, and inflammation. Must be addressed as part of treatment.

Treatment of Class II/2
1

CRITICAL FIRST STEP — Procline the Upper Centrals

Before any other treatment: the upper central incisors MUST be proclined (tipped labially). Reason: if you try to correct Class II with a functional appliance while centrals are retroclined, the centrals will be proclined during FA treatment but then the tight lower lip will push them back. Procline centrals first → makes the malocclusion temporarily look worse → but sets up for correct treatment. A palatal spring on a URA or upper removable appliance is used first.

2

Address the Deep Overbite

Anterior bite plane (URA) or levelling curve of Spee with fixed appliance. The deep bite must be reduced before or simultaneously with other treatment.

3

Class II Correction (If Growing)

Once upper incisors proclined and OB controlled: functional appliance or fixed mechanics for Class II correction. Same approach as Class II/1 at this stage.

4

Fixed Appliance Detailing

Full fixed appliance. Careful torque prescription needed for centrals (may need high torque brackets). Upper arch expansion may be needed (Class II/2 often has narrow arch).

Why Class II/2 Is Different

  • Arch form: square/narrow upper arch often needs expansion
  • Strong genetic component: manage patient expectations about long-term stability
  • Deep bite: major challenge — requires prolonged retention with anterior bite plane or fixed retainer

Genetic (Most Important)

Facemask (Delaire Mask)
  • Indication: Class III due to maxillary deficiency (reduced SNA) in patients <10yr ideally

  • Wear: 12–14 hours/day minimum

Orthognathic Surgery — The Surgical Options
BSSO — Bilateral Sagittal Split Osteotomy

Mandibular setback. The mandible is split sagittally through the ramus on both sides → repositioned backward → fixed with titanium plates and screws. Most commonly used for Class III mandibular prognathism. Can move mandible back by up to 10–12mm safely.

Le Fort I Osteotomy

Maxillary advancement. The maxilla is cut horizontally above the tooth roots and moved forward. Used when maxillary deficiency is the primary cause. Can advance maxilla up to 10mm. Often combined with BSSO for combined maxillomandibular correction.

Pre-Surgical Decompensation — COUNTERINTUITIVE!
PRE-SURGICAL ORTHODONTICS — THE PARADOX

Before orthognathic surgery, the teeth must be DECOMPENSATED. This means: procline the lower incisors (which were naturally retroclined as dental compensation) and retrocline the upper incisors. This makes the reverse OJ WORSE and the Class III looks more severe. This is correct and necessary! Once the jaws are surgically repositioned, the teeth will be in the correct position. If you don't decompensate first, after surgery the teeth will have nowhere to go.

GROWTH COMPLETION CRITERIA
  • Girls: Growth complete ~18 years (can operate from ~17–18yr)
  • Boys: Mandibular growth can continue until 20–22 years → surgery deferred until then
  • Growth assessment: serial cephalograms 6 months apart → no change = complete

RETENTION PRINCIPLE

Every orthodontic case requires retention. The periodontal ligament fibres, facial musculature, and ongoing dental development all exert forces that tend to move teeth toward their original positions. Without retention, some degree of relapse occurs in virtually all cases.

Types of Retainers
Removable Retainers
  • Hawley retainer: Acrylic plate + labial bow (0.7mm SS wire). Can be adjusted. Allows teeth to settle into occlusion. Durable. Shown to allow some overbite relapse.
  • Vacuum-formed / Essix retainer: Clear thermoplastic (Essix, Zendura). Full coverage of all teeth. Less visible. Patient-preferred. Provides more control. Does NOT allow natural settling of occlusion.

  • Lower 3-3 bonded wire: Multi-strand stainless steel wire bonded to the lingual surfaces of the lower canines (and sometimes all 6 anterior teeth). Most reliable — patient cannot remove it.
  • Upper 3-3 bonded wire: Less common. Used when upper incisor position particularly unstable.
  • Spring retainer: For single tooth retention where minimal retention elsewhere is needed.
Duration of Retention
BRITISH ORTHODONTIC SOCIETY POSITION (2013)

The BOS guidance states: patients should be advised that orthodontic retention should be for life if they wish to maintain their result long-term. Teeth naturally drift throughout life regardless of orthodontic treatment. The concept of "2 year retention then discharge" is outdated. Patients must be consented for this before treatment begins — this is a key medico-legal point.

Fibrotomy — Reducing Rotation Relapse
Pericision / Circumferential Supracrestal Fibrotomy (CSF)

Surgical cutting of the supracrestal periodontal fibres around a rotated tooth immediately before debond or at debond. Evidence shows this reduces rotational relapse significantly. A blade is inserted into the sulcus around the tooth circumferentially, cutting the gingival fibres. Does NOT cut the interdental fibres or bone. Requires LA. Takes 5–10 minutes per tooth.

Ortho-Periodontal
PERIODONTAL RULE — STABLE BEFORE ORTHO

The periodontium must be stable and disease-free before orthodontic treatment begins. Orthodontic forces in an inflamed periodontium dramatically accelerate bone loss. BOP (bleeding on probing) should be <10–15% before banding. Any active perio must be treated and re-evaluated before orthodontics.

Reduced Periodontium — Special Considerations
  • Patients with bone loss: orthodontics still possible but lighter forces (35–50g for tipping, not standard)
  • Centre of resistance shifts apically → tipping increases
  • More frequent review (every 4 weeks vs 6-8 weeks)
  • Fixed retainers especially important post-treatment
  • Never move teeth into areas of poor bone support
Crown Lengthening — Before or After?
  • Crown lengthening to expose more tooth for restoration: do AFTER ortho if ortho will change gingival levels
  • Crown lengthening for biologic width violation: before ortho if margin placement is the issue
  • Wait 3 months minimum after crown lengthening before ortho (tissue must stabilise)
Ortho-Surgical (SARPE)
SARPE — Surgically Assisted Rapid Palatal Expansion
  • Indication: Significant maxillary transverse deficiency in ADULTS where midpalatal suture is fused (>16–17yr)
  • Procedure: Surgical release of midpalatal suture and pterygomaxillary junction under GA → then RPE device activated post-operatively
  • Advantage: Allows bone-borne expansion without dental tipping
  • vs Alternative: MARPE (Miniscrew-Assisted RPE) — newer alternative; less invasive but more limited
  • Post-SARPE: full fixed treatment to detail occlusion
Forced Eruption (Orthodontic Extrusion)
Indications & Protocol
  • Indications: Subgingival fracture, short clinical crown, previous crown failure, root caries below gingival margin
  • Protocol: Bond bracket/button to tooth → apply extrusion force (25–50g) with elastic → tooth erupts coronally bringing bone and gingiva with it → after extrusion: crown lengthening to correct gingival levels → restoration
  • Benefit: Converts a tooth previously requiring extraction into a maintainable tooth
  • Limitation: Doesn't work if root length becomes inadequate, or if fracture extends subosseous

The CLAP Framework
CLAP — SYSTEMATIC TREATMENT PLANNING

CLAP provides a structured way to address every dimension of a malocclusion before committing to treatment. Work through each component systematically: C = Crowding/space; L = Lower labial segment; A = Anteroposterior (class); P = Profile. Never plan treatment without addressing all four.

C — Crowding & Space Analysis
Questions to Answer Under "C"
  • Is there crowding or spacing? Severity (LII / ALD)?
  • Are extractions needed? Which teeth? Upper only, lower only, or both arches?
  • How will space be created? Extraction vs IPR vs expansion vs distalization?
  • Asymmetric crowding → asymmetric extractions?
  • Missing teeth — open or close spaces?
  • Bolton discrepancy present? Tooth size mismatch?
  • Third molar assessment — extract or retain?
L — Lower Labial Segment
THE LOWER INCISOR RULE

The position of the lower labial segment (LI) is the key determinant of treatment plan. Lower incisors are the most difficult to move and the most likely to relapse. The golden rule: plan treatment around where the lower incisors need to be, then make everything else fit.

A — Anteroposterior (Skeletal Class)
Addressing the AP Component
  • What is the skeletal class? (ANB angle, Wits, clinical assessment)
  • Can it be corrected with growth modification? → Growing patient + functional appliance
  • If no growth: camouflage possible? → Assess limits of camouflage (tooth inclination, profile)
  • If severe: orthognathic surgery? → Await growth completion, plan with surgeon
  • The AP problem drives the extraction pattern and mechanics
  • Class I: symmetrical extractions if needed for crowding
  • Class II: upper premolars first; lower premolars if lower crowding also present
  • Class III: lower premolars for camouflage; or no extractions + surgery
P — Profile
PROFILE ASSESSMENT — THE FINAL CHECK

Every treatment plan must be assessed for its effect on the facial profile. Will the planned tooth movement improve, worsen, or not affect the profile? Profile assessment prevents creating a technically correct occlusion but an aesthetically poor facial result.

Putting CLAP Together — A Case Example
Example: 13yr girl, Class II/1, OJ 8mm, moderate crowding both arches, convex profile
  • C: Moderate crowding both arches → extractions likely. Upper 4s + Lower 4s OR upper 4s only post-FA.
  • L: Lower incisors reasonably placed (+1mm APg) → do not need to move them significantly → upper 4s only post-FA may suffice for lower crowding if only mild.
  • A: Class II/1, OJ 8mm, ANB 6°, growing (CS2-3) → Twin Block first → correct to Class I → then fixed upper 4 extraction.
  • P: Convex profile → retracting upper labial segment with extraction is GOOD for profile → confirms extraction plan.
  • Plan: Twin Block (6–12 months) → Upper 4 extractions → Upper + lower fixed appliances (18 months) → Retention (lifelong).

Benefits of Orthodontic Treatment
Clinical Benefits
  • Correction of traumatic occlusion (OB/OJ causing gingival/tooth damage)
  • Reduction of trauma risk (OJ >6mm → high trauma risk corrected)
  • Facilitation of restorative work (implants, crowns, bridges)
  • Improvement of oral hygiene access (crowding → easier cleaning)
  • Function (severe Class III: chewing and speech improvement)
  • Correction of impacted teeth (prevent resorption of adjacent roots)
Quality of Life Benefits
  • Psychological well-being: self-confidence, reduced social anxiety
  • Reduced teasing/bullying (especially children with severe Class II/1 or prognathism)
  • Improved dental aesthetics: smile confidence
  • Patient-reported satisfaction consistently high post-treatment
  • Long-term: sustained quality of life improvement in appropriately selected cases
EARR — External Apical Root Resorption in Detail
Risk Factors for Significant EARR
  • High-risk tooth shapes: pipette/pointed, dilacerated, bent roots
  • Previous trauma history to the tooth
  • Intrusion and bodily movement mechanics (more than tipping)
  • Long treatment duration (>24 months)
  • Heavy forces
  • Genetic predisposition (family history of short roots)
  • Class II with excessive upper incisor movement
MANAGEMENT IF EARR DETECTED
  • <2mm: continue with monitoring. PA every 6 months.
  • 2–4mm: reassess. Consider altering mechanics or reducing force. Discuss with patient.
  • >4mm: seriously consider stopping treatment. Remaining root length must be sufficient for tooth survival. If tooth root is <⅓ of original length → very poor prognosis. Involve patient in decision.
Informed Consent — What Must Be Disclosed
Consent Checklist — Fixed Appliances
  • Duration of treatment (usually 18–24 months for full fixed)
  • Root resorption risk (average 1mm; severe in small %)
  • White spot lesions / caries risk if OH poor
  • Relapse — lifelong retention required, implications of not wearing
  • Pain and discomfort (normal after each wire change)
  • Soft tissue trauma (brackets, wires)
  • Risk of bracket breakage (diet advice needed)
  • No guarantee of perfect result (biological variability)
  • Need for extractions (risks of extraction if applicable)
  • TMD: treatment does not cause TMD but patient may develop it independently
  • Document everything: signed consent form, discussion recorded in notes

White Spot Lesions — Prevention & Management
1

Prevention — Before Bonding

Assess caries risk (CAMBRA). If caries-active: delay bonding until risk controlled. Apply fluoride varnish at bonding appointment. Thorough OH instruction with interdental brushes and fluoride toothpaste.

2

During Treatment

5000ppm fluoride toothpaste. Professional fluoride varnish at every review appointment. Reinforce OH at EVERY appointment — never assume patient is compliant. Consider fluoride-releasing bonding composite for high-risk patients.

3

Decision to Debond Early

If OH repeatedly poor and WSL are forming/worsening: debond. Incomplete treatment is better than irreversible tooth damage. Discuss with patient and orthodontist.

4

Post-Debond WSL Management

Allow 6 months of remineralisation with high-dose fluoride. Tooth Mousse (CPP-ACP). Microabrasion if WSL remains visible. Icon resin infiltration (excellent for subsurface WSL). Composite masking for severe cases.

  • True allergy: use titanium mini-screws, ceramic brackets, nickel-free SS or titanium archwires
