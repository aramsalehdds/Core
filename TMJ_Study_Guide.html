Temporomandibular Joint

The most complex and uniquely structured joint in the human body — a bilateral, synovial, diarthrodial joint connecting the mandibular condyle to the temporal bone.

⚠️ What Makes TMJ Unique The TMJ is unlike any other joint in the body for several critical reasons: (1) bilateral joints working in perfect synchrony, (2) articular surfaces covered by fibrocartilage — not hyaline cartilage, (3) interpositional articular disc divides the joint into two compartments, (4) both hinge AND gliding movements occur, (5) it is the only joint in the body where the two sides cannot move independently.

Components at a Glance

🦴 Bony Components

  • Mandibular condyle — convex head
  • Mandibular fossa — concave glenoid fossa
  • Articular eminence — the load-bearing surface
  • Postglenoid process — limits posterior displacement
  • Tympanic plate — posterior boundary

💿 Soft Tissue Components

  • Articular disc (meniscus) — fibrocartilaginous, biconcave
  • Fibrous capsule — surrounds joint completely
  • Synovial membrane — lines capsule (not disc/articular surfaces)
  • Ligaments — lateral, sphenomandibular, stylomandibular
  • Bilaminar zone — posterior disc attachment

💪 Muscles of Mastication

  • Masseter — primary closer, most superficial
  • Temporalis — fan-shaped, closes + retrudes
  • Medial pterygoid — closes + protrudes
  • Lateral pterygoid — opens + protrudes (two heads)

All innervated by CN V3 (mandibular nerve)

⚡ Neural & Vascular Supply

  • Sensory: Auriculotemporal nerve (posterior), Masseteric nerve (anterior)
  • Motor: Anterior division CN V3
  • Primary artery: Maxillary artery (from external carotid)
  • Secondary: Superficial temporal artery

Clinical Significance

🔵 Why You Must Know This Temporomandibular disorders (TMD) affect approximately 5–12% of the population and are the most common orofacial pain condition seen in clinical dentistry. TMD encompasses muscle disorders, disc displacements, and degenerative joint diseases. Mastery of TMJ anatomy is essential for diagnosis, examination, imaging interpretation, and treatment planning.
📌 Fibrocartilage vs. Hyaline Cartilage — Why It Matters The TMJ articular surfaces (and disc) are covered by fibrocartilage (type I collagen), not hyaline cartilage. Fibrocartilage is avascular and aneural — it can withstand compressive loading. It has greater capacity for repair than hyaline cartilage. This also explains why TMJ articular changes appear differently on MRI compared to other joints.

Bony Anatomy of the TMJ

The osseous framework forming the articulation between the mandible and the temporal bone of the skull base.

Mandibular Condyle

The mandibular condyle is the superior projection of the mandibular ramus, forming the movable part of the TMJ.

Morphology

  • Convex, ellipsoid shape
  • Width (mediolateral): 15–20 mm
  • Depth (anteroposterior): 8–10 mm
  • Long axis oriented medially and posteriorly (~15–30° from the frontal plane)
  • If both condyle axes are extended, they meet approximately at the foramen magnum
  • Superior surface covered by fibrocartilage (~0.5 mm thick)

Poles

  • Medial pole: larger, projects further medially; more prominent
  • Lateral pole: smaller; directly palpable anterior to tragus
  • Condylar neck below the condylar head
  • Pterygoid fovea: depression on anterior surface of condylar neck — insertion point of lateral pterygoid (inferior head)
⚠️ Clinical Pearl The lateral pole of the condyle is the most accessible palpation point. Place fingertips just anterior to the tragus and ask the patient to open — you will feel the condyle translate anteriorly. This is the primary landmark for TMJ palpation in clinical examination.

Temporal Bone Components

Mandibular Fossa (Glenoid Fossa)

  • Concave depression in squamous part of temporal bone
  • Located anterior to tympanic plate and postglenoid process
  • Posterior wall: tympanic plate (thin bone, closely related to middle ear)
  • Covered by thin fibrocartilage (non-load-bearing in health)
  • The fossa itself is NOT the primary load-bearing surface
  • Important: the condyle is NOT designed to bear load within the fossa at rest

Articular Eminence (Tubercle)

  • Convex bony ridge anterior to the mandibular fossa
  • Also called the articular tubercle
  • This IS the primary load-bearing surface
  • The condyle translates down the posterior slope of the eminence during opening
  • Posterior slope of the eminence = articular surface (steep, covered by thick fibrocartilage)
  • Its height determines the path of condylar movement — steeper eminence → more angled path

Postglenoid Process

  • Bony projection posterior to the mandibular fossa
  • Separates the fossa from the external auditory meatus
  • Functions as a mechanical stop to prevent excessive posterior condylar displacement
  • Fracture of the postglenoid process (e.g., in condylar fractures) can allow condyle to displace into the middle ear

Tympanic Plate

  • Forms posterior and inferior walls of external auditory meatus
  • Forms posterior wall of the mandibular fossa
  • Very thin bone (may be perforated)
  • Close proximity to condyle: accounts for referred otalgia (ear pain) in TMD
  • In ankylosis: condyle can erode through tympanic plate

Condylar Head Position

🔵 Normal vs. Abnormal Position In health: the condyle is centered or slightly anteriorly positioned within the fossa with the disc interposed between the condyle and articular eminence. Posterior condylar positioning may indicate retrodiscal tissue compression. Assessment of condylar position is done on CT or CBCT. Note: condylar position varies widely in asymptomatic individuals — avoid over-interpreting position alone.

Articular Disc, Capsule & Ligaments

The soft tissue architecture dividing the TMJ into two separate compartments, each with distinct biomechanical functions.

Articular Disc (Meniscus)

The TMJ disc is a fibrocartilaginous (type I and II collagen) structure that divides the joint into superior and inferior compartments.

Morphology

  • Biconcave in sagittal cross-section
  • Superior surface: concavo-convex (conforming to the temporal surface)
  • Inferior surface: concave (conforming to the condyle)
  • Avascular and aneural at its central (load-bearing) portion
  • Peripherally vascularized (where attached to capsule)

Zones (Anterior to Posterior)

  • Anterior band: thick (~2 mm); attaches to capsule and superior lateral pterygoid
  • Intermediate zone: thinnest (~1 mm); the LOAD-BEARING portion (between condyle and eminence)
  • Posterior band: thickest (~3 mm); superior to condyle at rest; attaches to bilaminar zone

Bilaminar Zone (Posterior Attachment / Retrodiscal Tissue)

The most critical region for understanding disc displacement pathology.

Upper Stratum (Superior Layer)

  • Composed of elastic fibers
  • Attaches disc to tympanic plate
  • Pulls disc posteriorly during condylar translation (opening)
  • Becomes taut as condyle moves forward

Lower Stratum (Inferior Layer)

  • Composed of collagen fibers (non-elastic)
  • Attaches disc to posterior condylar neck
  • Limits anterior disc excursion

Retrodiscal Tissue (Between Strata)

  • Highly vascularized and well-innervated
  • Contains loose connective tissue, fat, vessels (arteriovenous plexus)
  • Allows blood to enter/exit as condyle translates — acts as a hydraulic cushion
  • Clinical importance: When disc displaces anteriorly, this tissue becomes compressed between condyle and eminence → pain source in disc displacement disorders
🚨 Why Bilaminar Zone = Pain in Disc Displacement In anterior disc displacement without reduction: the disc fails to recapture, and the richly innervated retrodiscal tissue takes the compressive load between the condyle and articular eminence. This is the primary source of pain. Chronic loading leads to metaplasia of retrodiscal tissue → pseudo-disc formation.

Joint Compartments

Superior Joint Space

Between the disc and temporal bone (articular eminence/fossa)

  • Larger compartment
  • Function: Gliding / Translation
  • Condyle + disc complex glide forward together
  • Occurs in the second phase of mouth opening
  • Clinical: Arthrocentesis typically performed in this space

Inferior Joint Space

Between the disc and mandibular condyle

  • Smaller compartment
  • Function: Hinge / Rotation
  • Condyle rotates beneath the disc
  • Occurs in the first phase of mouth opening (~25 mm)
  • Clinical: Less commonly accessed surgically
🔑 Memory Tip: Two Spaces, Two Movements
  • Inferior = Inferior (hinge) = Rotation — "IR" = InferioR
  • Superior = Sliding = Translation — "SST" = Superior Slides & Translates
  • First 25 mm of opening: rotation (inferior space) | After 25 mm: translation (superior space)

Joint Capsule

  • Fibrous envelope completely surrounding the joint
  • Attaches above: margins of fossa and articular eminence
  • Attaches below: condylar neck
  • Reinforced laterally by the lateral ligament (temporomandibular ligament)
  • Inner surface lined by synovial membrane (except over articular surfaces and disc)
  • Synovial membrane produces synovial fluid (lubrication + nutrition)
  • Two layers of synovial membrane: intimal (lining) + subintimal (vascular)

The sphenomandibular ligament is especially important in local anesthesia: it is the landmark between the ramus (superficial) and the medial pterygoid muscle (deep) in the pterygomandibular space where IANB is administered.

Muscles of Mastication

Four muscles derived from the first pharyngeal arch, all innervated by the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve (CN V3).

📌 Key Principle: All 4 Muscles → ONE Nerve Every muscle of mastication is innervated by the mandibular nerve (CN V3). This is a fundamental anatomical principle and high-yield exam fact. All are first pharyngeal arch derivatives (Meckel's cartilage).
💪

Masseter

Most powerful masticatory muscle; primary jaw closer
Origin
Superficial head (larger): Lower border of anterior ⅔ of zygomatic arch and zygomatic bone
Deep head (smaller): Lower border AND medial surface of entire zygomatic arch (including posterior ⅓)
Insertion
Superficial head: Lateral surface of angle and lower ramus
Deep head: Upper ramus and lateral surface of coronoid process
Action
Primary: Elevation of mandible (jaw closure) — most powerful closer
Secondary: Superficial head assists protrusion; deep head assists retrusion
Innervation
Masseteric nerve (branch of anterior division of CN V3)
Enters deep surface of muscle through the mandibular (sigmoid) notch
Blood Supply
Masseteric artery (branch of maxillary artery) — main supply
Transverse facial artery (superficial temporal artery) — superficial supply
Clinical
Most commonly hypertrophied in bruxism (jaw clenching); palpated directly over the cheek; primary Botox target for masseteric hypertrophy; trismus; "masseteric sling" formed with medial pterygoid
🌀

Temporalis

Large fan-shaped muscle; unique ability to retrude the mandible
Origin
Floor of the temporal fossa (temporal bone, parietal bone, greater wing of sphenoid, frontal bone) between the superior and inferior temporal lines; deep surface of temporal fascia
Insertion
Coronoid process of mandible: tip, apex, anterior & posterior borders, and medial surface; extends inferiorly to anterior border of ramus (retromolar triangle area)
Action
All fibers: Elevation (jaw closure)
Anterior vertical fibers: Elevation only
Posterior horizontal fibers: Retrusion — the ONLY significant retruder
Note: unilateral contraction → lateral movement (ipsilateral)
Innervation
Deep temporal nerves (anterior & posterior deep temporal nerves, sometimes middle)
Branches of the anterior division of CN V3
Blood Supply
Deep temporal arteries (anterior & posterior; branches of maxillary artery)
Middle temporal artery (from superficial temporal artery)
Clinical
Temporal headaches; trismus assessed at temporalis; most common site of referred pain from TMD; palpated in temporal fossa; coronoidectomy may be needed in severe trismus/ankylosis; important anatomical corridor for temporal flap in reconstruction
🔺

Medial Pterygoid

"Inside masseter" — forms the pterygoid sling with masseter
Origin
Deep head (main): Medial surface of lateral pterygoid plate of sphenoid bone
Superficial head (small): Pyramidal process of palatine bone AND maxillary tuberosity
Insertion
Medial surface of mandibular ramus and angle of mandible (roughened medial pterygoid tuberosity — inferior to mandibular foramen)
Action
Bilateral: Elevation (jaw closure) and protrusion
Unilateral: Contralateral (lateral) excursion — moves mandible to OPPOSITE side
Works synergistically with masseter as the "masseteric sling"
Innervation
Medial pterygoid nerve (from main trunk of CN V3 — undivided portion, BEFORE it divides)
Also supplies tensor tympani and tensor veli palatini via the otic ganglion
Blood Supply
Pterygoid branches of maxillary artery
Clinical
The pterygomandibular space lies between medial pterygoid and medial surface of ramus — key landmark for IANB anesthetic; spasm → trismus; intraoral abscess in this space; forms "sling" with masseter suspending the mandibular angle
↔️

Lateral Pterygoid

The ONLY opener & protruder — most clinically important in TMD
Origin
Superior head (smaller): Infratemporal surface (crest) of greater wing of sphenoid bone
Inferior head (larger): Lateral surface of lateral pterygoid plate of sphenoid bone
Insertion
Superior head: Anterior aspect of articular disc and fibrous capsule of TMJ
Inferior head: Pterygoid fovea on anterior surface of condylar neck
Mnemonic: Superior head → Superior structure (disc); Inferior head → Inferior structure (condyle)
Action
Inferior head (active during OPENING): Pulls condyle forward → depression (opening) of mandible; bilateral action = depression + protrusion
Superior head (active during CLOSING/clenching): Counter-intuitive! Active during closing — pulls disc forward and stabilizes it as condyle translates back; controls/brakes the disc
Unilateral (both heads): Contralateral lateral excursion
Innervation
Lateral pterygoid nerve (from anterior division of CN V3)
Note: This branch comes from anterior division (not medial pterygoid nerve)
Blood Supply
Pterygoid branches of maxillary artery
Clinical
Superior head dysfunction → anterior disc displacement; difficult to palpate (requires intraoral approach, superior to maxillary tuberosity); key role in disc coordination; trismus; tender in TMD; the most significant muscle in internal derangement pathogenesis
🚨 HIGH YIELD: Superior Head of Lateral Pterygoid The superior head is unique among ALL muscles of mastication: it is active during CLOSING (eccentric function), NOT during opening. This is the opposite of what one might expect. Its role is to pull the disc anteriorly to stabilize it on the condyle as the condyle translates posteriorly during closing. Dysfunction of this mechanism = disc displacement.

Accessory Muscles of Depression (Jaw Openers)
  • Digastric (anterior belly: CN V3; posterior belly: CN VII) — pulls mandible down via hyoid
  • Mylohyoid (CN V3) — elevates hyoid, assists in opening
  • Geniohyoid (C1 via CN XII) — pulls hyoid forward and mandible down
  • Stylohyoid (CN VII) — elevates hyoid
These suprahyoid muscles can only depress the mandible when the hyoid is fixed inferiorly by the infrahyoid muscles.

Innervation

The trigeminal nerve (CN V) — specifically its mandibular division (V3) — provides both sensory and motor innervation to the TMJ region.

Trigeminal Nerve Overview

V1 — Ophthalmic

Sensory only
Foramen: Superior orbital fissure
Supplies: Orbit, forehead, nose

Not directly involved in TMJ/mastication

V2 — Maxillary

Sensory only
Foramen: Foramen rotundum
Supplies: Cheek, palate, upper teeth

Pain from upper teeth can refer to TMJ area

V3 — Mandibular ★

Mixed (motor + sensory)
Foramen: Foramen ovale
ALL muscles of mastication

Primary nerve of TMJ!

Posterior Division — Mostly Sensory

Sensory: TMJ posterior aspect (primary TMJ nerve), external ear, scalp, parotid; parasympathetic secretomotor to parotid (via otic ganglion fibers from CN IX); splits around middle meningeal artery

TMJ-Specific Innervation

Auriculotemporal Nerve

Primary sensory nerve to the TMJ — provides the majority of joint innervation

  • Innervates posterior & lateral capsule
  • Retrodiscal tissue
  • Lateral & posterior surfaces of disc
  • Also: External auditory meatus, tragus, pre-auricular skin, temporal scalp
  • Explains why TMJ pain is often felt in the ear

Masseteric Nerve

Provides innervation to anterior & medial aspects of TMJ capsule

  • From anterior division of CN V3
  • Passes through sigmoid notch
  • Also motor to masseter
  • Anterior disc innervation

Deep temporal nerves and lateral pterygoid nerve also contribute minimally

⚠️ Common Exam Trap — Buccinator Innervation The buccinator muscle is NOT innervated by the buccal nerve (CN V3). The buccal nerve is purely SENSORY. Buccinator is innervated by the buccal branch of the FACIAL nerve (CN VII). This is one of the most commonly confused anatomy facts in dental exams.

Autonomic Innervation & the Otic Ganglion

Otic Ganglion

  • Parasympathetic ganglion located just below foramen ovale
  • Preganglionic: CN IX (glossopharyngeal) → tympanic plexus → lesser petrosal nerve → otic ganglion
  • Postganglionic fibers hitchhike on auriculotemporal nerve → parotid gland (secretomotor)
  • Also carries sympathetic and sensory fibers
Frey Syndrome (Auriculotemporal Syndrome) After parotidectomy: aberrant regeneration of parasympathetic fibers (meant for parotid) into sweat gland innervation (sympathetic territory) → gustatory sweating (sweating of the cheek when eating). Treated with Botox or tympanic neurectomy.

Blood Supply

The TMJ and muscles of mastication are supplied predominantly by branches of the external carotid artery, primarily via the maxillary artery.

Overview of Arterial Supply

External Carotid Artery → Main Source

The external carotid artery gives 8 terminal/collateral branches. Two are relevant here:

  • Maxillary artery (terminal branch) — primary supply to deep masticatory muscles and TMJ

Arises behind the mandibular neck within the parotid gland. Passes through the infratemporal fossa. Three parts: mandibular, pterygoid, pterygopalatine.

Biomechanics of the TMJ

The TMJ undergoes two distinct types of movement: rotation (hinge) and translation (glide), occurring in a coordinated sequence during jaw opening.

Two-Phase Jaw Opening Mechanics

Phase 1: Rotation (~0–25 mm)

  • Occurs in the inferior joint space
  • Condyle rotates beneath the disc
  • Disc and condyle remain relatively stationary (disc does not translate)
  • Pure hinge movement
  • Muscles active: Lateral pterygoid inferior head + suprahyoids
  • Produces approximately the first 25 mm of interincisal opening

Phase 2: Translation (~25–50+ mm)

  • Occurs in the superior joint space
  • Condyle + disc complex translate forward together
  • Condyle slides down the posterior slope of the articular eminence
  • Disc is pulled posteriorly by elastic upper stratum of bilaminar zone
  • Muscles: Lateral pterygoid inferior head + suprahyoids continue
  • Full opening: condyle reaches anterior slope of articular eminence
⚙️ Disc Coordination During Opening As the condyle translates anteriorly, the disc is pulled POSTERIORLY by the elastic upper stratum (retraction). The lateral pterygoid (superior head, already tense from closing) gradually relaxes. The posterior band of the disc should remain on TOP of the condyle throughout. In disc displacement, the disc fails to remain coordinated and slips anteriorly off the condyle.

Mandibular Deviation Patterns

Deviation

Mandible deflects during opening but returns to midline at maximum opening.

  • S-shaped or C-shaped path
  • Typically: disc displacement WITH reduction (reciprocal clicking)
  • Condyle "catches" the disc and opens normally after click

Deflection

Mandible deflects during opening and does NOT return to midline — stays toward affected side.

  • Deflects toward the RESTRICTED side
  • Typically: disc displacement WITHOUT reduction (closed lock)
  • Or: unilateral ankylosis
  • Contralateral lateral excursion will also be limited
🔵 Mandibular Movement in Lateral Excursion During lateral excursion (e.g., chewing), two different movements occur simultaneously:
  • Working side (toward which mandible moves): condyle rotates around a vertical axis (Bennett movement) — ipsilateral muscles contract
  • Balancing side (non-working): condyle translates forward and medially — contralateral lateral pterygoid is the key muscle
The Bennett angle describes the lateral shift of the mandible during lateral movement. Important for dental articulator settings.

Clinical Examination of the TMJ

A systematic approach covering history, inspection, palpation, range of motion, auscultation, and special tests.

History Taking

Chief Complaint & Pain Assessment

  • Location: preauricular, temple, cheek, ear, teeth (referred)
  • Quality: aching, sharp, throbbing, dull
  • Onset: sudden (trauma) vs. gradual
  • Duration & pattern: constant vs. episodic
  • Modifying factors: chewing, talking, yawning, morning vs. evening
  • VAS score (0–10)

Joint Symptoms

  • Sounds: clicking, popping, crepitus, grating
  • Function: limited opening, locking (open or closed), catching
  • Locking pattern: morning stiffness, intermittent, progressive
  • Referred symptoms: otalgia, tinnitus, aural fullness, headache

Parafunctional Habits (KEY)

  • Bruxism (grinding — usually nocturnal)
  • Clenching (diurnal or nocturnal)
  • Nail-biting, pen-chewing
  • Unilateral chewing preference
  • Postural habits (chin resting on hand)

Relevant History

  • Trauma: blow to chin, whiplash
  • Dental: recent extractions, new restorations, orthodontic treatment
  • Medical: RA, psoriasis, gout, Ehlers-Danlos, fibromyalgia
  • Psychological: anxiety, depression, stress
  • Previous TMD treatment

Inspection

Extraoral Inspection

  • Facial symmetry (asymmetry → muscle hypertrophy, condylar hyperplasia, ankylosis)
  • Swelling over TMJ (preauricular)
  • Masseter hypertrophy (bilateral fullness of cheeks)
  • Mandibular midline position
  • Deviation/deflection during opening (observe from front)
  • Profile: retrognathia, prognathia, deep bite, open bite
  • Muscle bulk: temporalis wasting (neurological)

Intraoral Inspection

  • Wear facets: attrition from bruxism (matching facets on opposing teeth)
  • Abfraction: cervical non-carious lesions (tooth flexure from clenching)
  • Scalloped tongue: tongue pressure (clenching habit)
  • Linea alba: buccal mucosa ridge (cheek biting)
  • Tori (mandibular/palatal): associated with parafunctional habits
  • Occlusion: open bite, deep bite, cross-bite, Angle classification
  • Missing teeth, worn restorations, dentures

Palpation

TMJ Palpation

Place fingertips bilaterally, assess with mouth closed then during opening/closing. Grade tenderness 0–3.

Lateral approach: fingertips 1 cm anterior to tragus, just below zygomatic arch → palpate lateral condylar pole

Posterior approach (via EAC): little finger gently inserted into external auditory canal → assess posterior condylar movement and tenderness of posterior attachment

What to feel for:

  • Tenderness (preauricular, posterior)
  • Condylar translation (smooth vs. jerky)
  • Clicking/popping felt through finger
  • Asymmetric movement
  • Reduced translation (<10 mm forward movement = restriction)

Auscultation

Click / Pop

Single, distinct sound during opening or closing

  • Reciprocal clicking: opening click + closing click = disc displacement WITH reduction
  • Opening click: condyle recaptures anteriorly displaced disc
  • Closing click: disc slips off again
  • Reciprocal click disappears on protrusive opening

Crepitus

Continuous grating, grinding sound throughout movement

  • Like gravel/sand sound
  • Indicates: degenerative joint disease (OA/DJD)
  • Rough articular surfaces rubbing
  • Heard AND felt during palpation
  • May also: perforation of disc

Subluxation Pop

Loud single sound near end of opening range

  • Condyle moving over articular eminence
  • Hypermobility
  • Not necessarily painful
  • Different from disc-related click (occurs at different point in range)
🎯 Key Clinical Correlation: Sound → Diagnosis
  • Reciprocal click (opening + closing) → Disc displacement with reduction (DDwR)
  • Single late opening click only → Hypermobility
  • Crepitus throughout range → Degenerative joint disease (DJD/OA)
  • No sound + limited opening + deviation toward affected side → Disc displacement WITHOUT reduction (DDwoR) — "closed lock"
  • No sound + normal opening → Myofascial pain or arthralgia (normal joint mechanics)

Red Flags — Refer / Investigate

!
Progressive trismus without clear cause — rule out malignancy (pterygoid plate involvement, NPC, parotid malignancy)
!
Rapid progressive unilateral facial asymmetry — condylar hyperplasia (active growth), neoplasm
!
Numbness/paraesthesia of the lip or chin — IAN involvement; rule out malignancy (numb chin syndrome)
!

!
Night sweats, weight loss, constitutional symptoms — systemic disease, malignancy
!
Pulsatile TMJ mass / vascular sounds — vascular anomaly (AVF, AVM)

TMJ Dislocation

Acute Dislocation

Condyle moves anterior to the articular eminence and cannot self-reduce. Patient presents with mouth open and unable to close.

  • Causes: wide yawning, dental extraction, seizure, trauma, endoscopy
  • Bilateral more common than unilateral
  • Patient: chin protrudes, cannot close mouth, masticatory muscles in spasm

Management (Hippocratic Technique)

  • Patient seated, clinician stands in front
  • Thumbs on mandibular molar region (protected with gauze)
  • Fingers under chin
  • Apply: downward pressure (depress condyle), then posterior pressure (push back into fossa)
  • LA (auriculotemporal block) and/or diazepam may be needed
  • Post-reduction: soft diet, avoid wide opening, chin bandage if recurrent

Chronic Recurrent Dislocation / Hypermobility

Repeated episodes of condyle passing anterior to eminence

  • Risk factors: ligamentous laxity (Ehlers-Danlos), psychiatric patients on phenothiazines, shallow eminence
  • May become habitual (subluxation = partial dislocation, self-reducing)

Surgical Options

  • Eminoplasty: augment height of articular eminence (bone graft or alloplast)
  • Eminectomy: remove articular eminence → no mechanical block
  • Lateral pterygoid myotomy: reduce protrusive force
  • Capsulorrhaphy: tighten capsule
  • Botox to lateral pterygoid: reduce translation (newer approach)

Ankylosis

Bony or fibrous union between condyle and temporal bone, resulting in restricted or absent jaw movement.

Classification

  • True ankylosis: intra-articular fusion (bony or fibrous)
  • False (extra-articular) ankylosis: bony/fibrous union outside the joint (coronoid/zygomatic arch fusion; masseter fibrosis; depressed zygomatic fracture)
  • Fibrous: early; some residual movement; no bony union on CT
  • Bony: complete immobility; CT shows bone bridging fossa and condyle

Causes

  • Trauma (most common in adults): subcondylar fractures in children
  • Infection: septic arthritis, otitis media spread
  • Systemic: RA, ankylosing spondylitis, psoriatic arthritis
  • Post-surgical / post-radiation

Management

  • Coronoidectomy (always) + gap arthroplasty or condylar reconstruction
  • Total joint replacement for severe cases
  • Aggressive post-operative physiotherapy (key!)

MRI of the TMJ — Key Concepts

Normal MRI Findings

  • Disc: low signal (dark) on both T1 and T2 — fibrocartilage
  • Normal disc position (closed mouth): posterior band of disc at 12 o'clock position on condyle (superior); intermediate zone between condyle and eminence
  • Superior joint space: thin fluid rim (normal)
  • Retrodiscal tissue: intermediate to high signal (vascular)
  • Condylar marrow: normal fatty marrow signal

Abnormal MRI Findings

  • Disc displacement: posterior band anterior to 12 o'clock (DDwR = recaptures on opening; DDwoR = does not)
  • Disc morphology: biconvex disc (not biconcave) = deformed/elongated
  • Effusion: high T2 signal in joint space
  • Bone marrow edema: high T2 signal in condyle — indicates active inflammation/OA
  • Osteonecrosis: geographic low T1/T2 signal in condyle
⚠️ Interpreting Disc Position The disc is classified as displaced when the posterior band is anterior to the 12 o'clock position in closed mouth MRI. Mild displacement: 11–12 o'clock. Moderate: 9–11 o'clock. Severe: less than 9 o'clock. However — correlate with clinical findings. Many asymptomatic individuals have MRI disc displacement. Do not treat the MRI alone.

Treatment of TMJ Disorders

A stepwise approach from least to most invasive — the majority of TMD is self-limiting and responds to conservative management.

✅ Core Principle: Conservative First 70–90% of TMD cases improve with reversible, conservative treatment alone. Surgery should be reserved for patients who fail adequate conservative management. Never perform irreversible treatment (irreversible occlusal adjustment, joint surgery) without a thorough trial of reversible conservative treatment.

Conservative (Non-Surgical)

Patient Education & Reassurance

  • Explain the benign nature of most TMD
  • Self-management: jaw rest, soft diet, avoid wide opening
  • Heat therapy: moist heat 20 min for muscle pain
  • Cold therapy (ice packs): acute joint inflammation
  • Parafunctional habit elimination counseling
  • Postural advice (forward head posture → cervical → TMD)
  • Sleep hygiene (impacts bruxism)

Physiotherapy

  • Jaw exercises: controlled opening/closing exercises
  • Myofascial release and trigger point therapy
  • TENS (Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation)
  • Ultrasound therapy (promotes healing)
  • Low-level laser therapy (LLLT)
  • Manual therapy: cervical manipulation if cervicogenic component
  • Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) for chronic pain
  • Biofeedback for bruxism management

Pharmacological Treatment

💊
NSAIDs (Ibuprofen 400–800mg TID, Naproxen 500mg BD)
Anti-inflammatory, analgesic. First-line for acute arthralgia and myalgia. Short-term 1–2 weeks. GI protection if prolonged. COX-2 inhibitors (celecoxib) if GI risk.
💊
Muscle Relaxants (Cyclobenzaprine 5–10mg at night, Methocarbamol)
Reduces muscle spasm and bruxism. Usually prescribed at night only. Short-term use. Caution: sedation, avoid in elderly, not for long-term use. Cyclobenzaprine is chemically related to TCAs.
💊
Tricyclic Antidepressants (Amitriptyline 10–25mg nocte)
Low-dose for chronic pain and sleep quality. Reduces central sensitization. Takes 4–6 weeks for full effect. SE: anticholinergic effects, sedation, weight gain.
💊
Benzodiazepines (Clonazepam 0.5mg at night)
For short-term bruxism, sleep disturbance, anxious patients. Very short term only (2–4 weeks) — risk of dependence. GABAergic mechanism reduces REM sleep bruxism.
💉
Corticosteroid Injection (Methylprednisolone, Triamcinolone intra-articular)
Direct anti-inflammatory into superior joint space. Used for acute synovitis, effusion, severe arthralgia. Limit to 3 injections/year — risk of articular cartilage damage. Guided by image if possible.
💉
Sodium Hyaluronate (Hyaluronic Acid) Injection
Viscosupplementation into superior joint space. Improves lubrication; anti-inflammatory; promotes disc repair. Often given as 3–5 weekly injections. Evidence: moderate benefit for DJD and internal derangements.
💉
Botulinum Toxin A (Botox) — Masseteric / Lateral Pterygoid
Masseter: 25–50 units per side for hypertrophy/bruxism; reduces muscle bulk (aesthetic and functional). Lateral pterygoid: reduces protrusive force in disc displacement. Duration 3–6 months; repeat as needed. Growing evidence base.

Surgical Treatment

Level 1: Arthrocentesis

Minimally invasive needle lavage of the superior joint space.

Technique

1
Mark landmarks

Tragus and lateral canthus — draw canthal-tragal line (CT line)

2

10 mm anterior to tragus along CT line = posterior needle (glenoid fossa); 20 mm anterior = anterior needle (eminence)

3

Inject 200–400 mL Ringer's lactate or saline through superior space; outflow through anterior needle

4
Hydraulic distension

Lyses adhesions; removes inflammatory mediators (cytokines, prostaglandins)

  • DDwoR (closed lock) — first-line surgical for acute closed lock
  • DJD/OA with inadequate conservative response
  • Inflammatory arthritis
  • Adhesions

Success Rate

  • ~70–80% success for closed lock
  • Advantages: minimal morbidity, day case, local anesthetic
  • May combine with corticosteroid or hyaluronate injection at end of procedure

Level 2: Arthroscopy

  • Endoscopic visualization and treatment of superior joint space
  • Diagnostic: assess disc, synovium, adhesions, articular surfaces
  • Therapeutic: lysis of adhesions, disc repositioning, discoplasty, synovectomy, lavage
  • Success rate 80–85%
  • Less morbidity than open surgery; GA required
  • Complications: facial nerve injury (rare), vascular injury, middle ear penetration

Complications

Recognizing and managing the sequelae of TMJ disorders and treatment interventions.

Ankylosis

  • Definition: Bony or fibrous fusion of condyle to temporal bone
  • Causes: subcondylar fracture (especially in children), infection, RA, post-surgical
  • Features: trismus, facial asymmetry, mandibular hypoplasia if in childhood
  • Classification: fibrous → bony; true (intra-articular) vs. false (extra-articular)
  • Management: gap arthroplasty + coronoidectomy + aggressive physio + TJR if severe
  • Key: physio post-op is as important as surgery — relapse rate without it is high

Growth Disturbance

  • In children: condyle = growth center; damage → mandibular hypoplasia
  • Causes: subcondylar fracture in child, juvenile RA (JIA), trauma, irradiation
  • Results: facial asymmetry, retrognathia, cross-bite, compensatory dental changes
  • Management: treat underlying cause + functional appliances (growth modification) + corrective orthognathic surgery at end of growth
  • Condylar hyperplasia (opposite): excess growth → facial asymmetry to OPPOSITE side
  • Assessment: bone scan + SPECT-CT to assess if growth is active

Progressive Condylar Resorption (PCR)

  • Also: Idiopathic condylar resorption (ICR)
  • Predominantly young females (teenage/young adult)
  • Progressive loss of condylar bone → class II skeletal pattern, anterior open bite
  • Often follows orthognathic surgery (post-surgical condylar resorption)
  • Risk factors: female, hormonal (estrogen receptor on condyle), anterior repositioning splints, posterior condylar loading
  • Treatment: observation, hormonal assessment, splint therapy; orthognathic + TJR when stabilized

Referred Pain Patterns

  • Otalgia: TMD pain referred to ear (via auriculotemporal nerve); may mimic otitis media
  • Tinnitus: via discomalleolar ligament (malleus connection)
  • Aural fullness: same mechanism
  • Temporal headache: temporalis muscle referred pain
  • Dental pain: myofascial trigger points can refer to teeth → unnecessary dental treatment risk
  • Cervical pain: convergence of C1-C3 and trigeminal at subnucleus caudalis

Iatrogenic Complications

Post-Arthrocentesis

  • Hematoma, infection, nerve injury (transient)
  • Middle ear penetration (if wrong angulation)

Post-Arthroscopy

  • Facial nerve injury (buccal branch most at risk)
  • Vascular injury (maxillary artery)
  • Broken instrument

Post-Open Surgery

  • Facial nerve palsy (preauricular approach: superficial temporal/frontal branches)
  • Heterotopic ossification (re-ankylosis)
  • Prosthesis failure/infection (TJR)
  • Frey syndrome (gustatory sweating)

Chronic Pain & Central Sensitization

  • Some patients develop chronic orofacial pain despite treatment
  • Central sensitization: amplified pain processing in CNS
  • Features: widespread pain, allodynia, hyperalgesia, poor sleep, psychological distress
  • Part of pain continuum with fibromyalgia, chronic widespread pain
  • Management: multidisciplinary pain clinic; CBT; low-dose TCA; pregabalin; pain psychology
  • Avoid repeated surgical interventions in centrally sensitized patients

Embryology of the TMJ

The TMJ is the last joint to develop in the human body and is unique in its embryological origin — it forms entirely from secondary cartilage, independent of Meckel's cartilage.

🔑 Key Concept: Secondary Cartilage Joint The TMJ is a SECONDARY cartilaginous joint — it develops entirely in membrane bone (intramembranous ossification). Unlike most other joints that develop from primary (Meckel's) cartilage, the TMJ forms from two independent condylar and temporal blastemas. The condylar cartilage is a SECONDARY cartilage (appears post-Meckel's, thinner, more fibrocellular).

Development Timeline

Weeks 6–7
First pharyngeal arch derivatives form. Meckel's cartilage develops — template for mandible. Mandible begins intramembranous ossification lateral to Meckel's cartilage.
Weeks 8–10
Condylar blastema (mesenchymal condensation) appears at superior ramus. Temporal blastema appears on temporal bone. These are INDEPENDENT from Meckel's cartilage.
Weeks 10–11
Secondary condylar cartilage appears within condylar blastema. Mandibular fossa begins forming in temporal bone. The secondary cartilage grows rapidly — responsible for most of condylar growth postnatally.
Weeks 12–13
Inferior joint space forms — cavitation within blastema between disc (forming) and condyle. Articular eminence begins to develop. Articular disc forms from vascularized mesenchyme between the two developing surfaces.
Weeks 14–15
Articular disc becomes distinct; lateral pterygoid fibers connect to anterior disc. Superior lateral pterygoid begins inserting into disc.
Weeks 16–17
Superior joint space forms — cavitation above the disc. Joint now has two distinct compartments. Capsule and ligaments differentiate. Bilaminar zone develops posteriorly.
Week 20
TMJ is structurally complete and functional. The joint begins loading as fetal jaw movements occur. Condylar growth continues postnatally as the primary adaptive growth site of the mandible.
Birth → ~20 years
Condylar growth continues — main mechanism of mandibular growth. Mandibular fossa is shallow at birth, deepens with tooth eruption and functional loading. Growth ceases with skeletal maturity (~18–20 years; slightly earlier in females).

Meckel's Cartilage — Fate and Significance

Meckel's cartilage is the primary cartilage of the first pharyngeal arch. Despite its importance, the TMJ does NOT develop FROM Meckel's cartilage — however, its perichondrium and remnants are clinically relevant.

Fate of Meckel's Cartilage

  • Middle third → regresses/disappears
  • Posterior portion → malleus and incus (ossicular chain, middle ear)
  • Perichondrium → sphenomandibular ligament and anterior malleolar ligament
  • A small portion contributes to anterior part of sphenoid

Clinical Relevance

  • Sphenomandibular ligament = remnant of Meckel's cartilage perichondrium
  • Discomalleolar ligament = connects disc to malleus → explains why ear symptoms occur in TMD
  • Pierre Robin sequence, Treacher Collins — first arch anomalies affecting TMJ development
  • Hemifacial microsomia — condylar/TMJ hypoplasia from first arch abnormality

Postnatal Condylar Growth

The condyle continues to grow postnatally as the primary site of mandibular growth. This is clinically critical for understanding when TMJ intervention can affect development.

  • Growth direction: posterosuperiorly (condyle grows up and back → mandible grows down and forward)
  • Periosteal resorption at anterior condylar neck + deposition at posterior condylar neck = bone drift forward
  • Condylar cartilage is an adaptive (pressure-responsive) growth site — unlike primary endochondral cartilage
  • Functional matrix theory (Moss): condyle responds to functional demands of the surrounding soft tissue matrix
  • Growth cessation: ~16–18 years in females; ~18–20 years in males (variable)
⚠️ Growth Center Considerations Damage to the condylar growth center (subcondylar fracture, JIA, radiation) in a child → mandibular hypoplasia and facial asymmetry. Orthognathic surgical correction should generally wait until growth is complete (verified by serial cephalometry and bone scan if uncertain). Condylar hyperplasia (excess growth) is different and may require early high condylectomy if asymmetry is progressing.

Clinical Pearls & Mnemonics

High-yield facts, memory aids, and exam tips for mastering TMJ and muscles of mastication.

Mnemonics

🔮 Muscles that CLOSE the jaw (Elevators)
"My Mommy Tends To..."

Masseter — Medial pterygoid — Temporalis
These 3 muscles close the jaw. The LATERAL pterygoid is the odd one out — it OPENS.
Alternative: "MMT" — Masseter, Medial pterygoid, Temporalis

🔮 Muscles that OPEN the jaw (Depressors)
"LDGM — Lateral Does Great Movements"

Lateral pterygoid (inferior head) — Digastric — Geniohyoid — Mylohyoid
Remember: the lateral pterygoid is the PRIMARY opener — all others are accessory via hyoid fixation.

🔮 Lateral Pterygoid Heads — Insertion
"Superior → Soft tissue (disc/capsule) | Inferior → Inferior condyle (fovea)"

Or: "S-S, I-I" = Superior head → Superior structure (disc); Inferior head → Inferior attachment (condyle/pterygoid fovea)

Function memory: "Superior Stops, Inferior Initiates" = Superior head is active during STOPPING (closing/braking); Inferior head INITIATES opening

🔮 Lateral Excursion — Which Side's Which?
"The Working side Works with its own muscles; the Balancing side needs its Lateral pterygoid"

Right excursion: Right TMJ = working side (rotation); RIGHT temporalis and masseter contract
Left TMJ = balancing side (translation); LEFT lateral pterygoid contracts to push condyle forward
Rule: OPPOSITE lateral pterygoid drives excursion

🔮 Joint Spaces and Movements
"I Rotate on the Inferior; Super Slides Superiorly"

Inferior space → Rotation (hinge)
Superior space → Sliding (translation/glide)
Think of it as a two-story joint: bottom floor rotates, top floor slides.

High-Yield Clinical Pearls

🚨 Pearl 1: TMJ Surface is FIBROCARTILAGE, not Hyaline This makes the TMJ different from almost all other synovial joints. Fibrocartilage (type I collagen) covers the condyle, articular eminence, and the disc. It is avascular, aneural, and more resistant to compressive forces. This also means it responds differently to treatment — fibrocartilage has greater repair capacity than hyaline.
🔵 Pearl 2: Articular Eminence ≠ Fossa is Load-Bearing The ARTICULAR EMINENCE (posterior slope) is the primary load-bearing surface of the TMJ — NOT the mandibular fossa. The fossa is thin and uncalcified. This explains why DJD changes (erosions, osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis) occur primarily on the condylar head and articular eminence, not within the fossa.
🌟 Pearl 3: Superior Head LP is Active During Closing Counter-intuitive: the superior head of the lateral pterygoid contracts during jaw CLOSING (and maximum intercuspation/clenching). It functions eccentrically to control disc position as the condyle translates back. This is WHY clenching/bruxism → superior head strain → disc displacement. Always remember this when asked about disc displacement pathogenesis.
⚡ Pearl 4: Sound-Diagnosis Correlation Clicking (reciprocal) = DDwR | Crepitus = DJD/OA | Silence + Limited opening = DDwoR (closed lock) | Silence + Normal opening = Myofascial or arthralgia. Reciprocal click goes away with protrusive opening = confirms DDwR.
✅ Pearl 5: Conservative First — Always 80–90% of TMD is self-limiting. Before any irreversible treatment: try soft diet, splint, NSAIDs, physio for at least 3–6 months. Never perform occlusal equilibration, orthognathic surgery, or TJR without adequate conservative trial. Do not treat the MRI alone.
🧬 Pearl 6: TMJ = Last Joint to Develop The TMJ is the last joint to complete development (Week 20 of embryology) and is the last to ossify postnatally. Condylar growth centers persist into the late teens/early twenties. Trauma to condyle in childhood → growth disruption → asymmetry. Plan orthognathic surgery only after growth cessation is confirmed.
⚠️ Pearl 7: Buccinator is CN VII, NOT CN V3 The buccal nerve (V3) is SENSORY only — it supplies sensation to the buccal mucosa and skin. The buccinator MUSCLE is supplied by the BUCCAL BRANCH of the FACIAL NERVE (CN VII). This is one of the most commonly incorrect answers in dental anatomy exams.
🚨 Pearl 8: Jaw Claudication → Rule Out Giant Cell Arteritis Jaw pain on chewing that goes away with rest = jaw claudication. This is the jaw equivalent of intermittent claudication. In elderly patients (especially female, >50 years), this suggests Giant Cell Arteritis (temporal arteritis). Check ESR urgently — this is a medical emergency because GCA can cause vision loss if untreated. Refer to rheumatology immediately.

🎯 High-Yield Exam Summary

  • All 4 muscles of mastication → CN V3 (mandibular nerve)
  • Only joint where articular surfaces = fibrocartilage (not hyaline)
  • Inferior space = rotation | Superior space = translation
  • Superior LP head active during CLOSING (braking disc); Inferior head during OPENING
  • Reciprocal click = DDwR | Crepitus = DJD | No click + limited = DDwoR
  • MRI for disc; CT/CBCT for bone; OPG for screening; Bone scan for active growth
  • Arthrocentesis = first surgical option for closed lock (acute DDwoR)
  • TMJ is the LAST joint to develop (complete ~20 weeks gestation)
  • Sphenomandibular ligament = remnant of Meckel's cartilage perichondrium
  • Jaw claudication in elderly = rule out Giant Cell Arteritis (ESR urgently)
  • Conservative treatment first — 80–90% TMD improves without surgery
