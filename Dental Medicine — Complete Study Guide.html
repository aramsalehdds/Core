📋 Patient History Taking

A thorough history is the foundation of dental diagnosis. Master the sequence: Chief Complaint → HPI → Medical History → Social History → Review of Systems.

Foundation skill SOCRATES Allergy & drug safety

Systematic History Sequence

Record in order: Chief Complaint → History of Present Illness → Past Dental History → Medical History → Drug History → Allergies → Family History → Social History → Review of Systems.
SOCRATES mnemonic — Site, Onset, Character, Radiation, Associations, Time course, Exacerbating/relieving factors, Severity (1–10). Use for ALL pain complaints.
History of Present Illness
Duration, progression, previous treatment, what makes it better or worse
Past Dental History
Visit frequency, previous procedures, anxiety level, previous complications
Medical History
Systemic conditions, medications, allergies, hospitalisations, surgeries
Family History
Hereditary conditions, dental anomalies, oral cancer risk
Never skip medication and allergy history. Drug interactions and allergic reactions can be life-threatening in a dental setting. Distinguish true allergy (immune-mediated) from intolerance/side effect.

Chief Complaint & History of Present Illness

Record the chief complaint in the patient's own words, in quotation marks. Then expand systematically using SOCRATES.
Pain assessment in detail ▾

Pulpal pain: Spontaneous, lancinating, poorly localised (especially upper teeth — referred pain), worse at night, initially relieved then prolonged by cold/heat.

Periodontal pain: Dull, throbbing, well-localised, tender to pressure, increased by chewing. No thermal symptoms.

Myofascial (TMD) pain: Dull ache, associated with jaw movement, headache, clicking sounds. Worse on waking (nocturnal bruxism).

Neuropathic pain: Burning, shooting, electric shock-like. Follows nerve distribution. Consider trigeminal neuralgia (paroxysmal, trigger zones), post-herpetic neuralgia, PDAP.

Always assess: Is the pain localised (periodontal) or diffuse (pulpal/referred)? Does it linger after thermal stimulus (>30s = irreversible pulpitis)?

Swelling assessment ▾

Rapid, painful, hard then fluctuant swelling = acute abscess. May have fever and lymphadenopathy.

Painless, slowly enlarging swelling = cyst, benign tumour, malignancy — needs investigation.

Bilateral swelling = salivary gland disease (Sjögren's), lymphoma, drug reaction, mumps, sarcoidosis.

Ask: duration, rate of growth, changes in size, pain, associated teeth, previous episodes, systemic symptoms (weight loss, night sweats = lymphoma alarm).

Ulcer assessment — the 3-week rule ▾

Duration is the single most important factor:

<3 weeks: Likely benign — traumatic ulcer, aphthous ulcer, herpetic. Manage conservatively and review.

>3 weeks without obvious cause: Must biopsy or urgently refer to exclude oral squamous cell carcinoma.

Also assess: number, size, site (lateral tongue and floor of mouth = highest risk), edge character (rolled/indurated = SCC), base, pain level (SCC often painless early).

Bleeding assessment ▾

Spontaneous vs provoked — spontaneous gingival bleeding suggests systemic cause.

Assess duration, extent, other bleeding sites (nosebleeds, bruising, menorrhagia).

Rule out: Blood dyscrasias (leukaemia, thrombocytopenia), anticoagulant therapy, bleeding disorders (haemophilia, Von Willebrand), liver disease.

Check: family history of bleeding problems, previous surgical/dental bleeding.

3-week rule: Any ulcer persisting beyond 3 weeks that doesn't have an obvious benign cause MUST be biopsied or urgently referred. Do not reassure and discharge without histological diagnosis.

🔍 Clinical Examination

Systematic extra- and intraoral examination, anatomic normal variants, and periodontal assessment. Never examine teeth before soft tissues.

Systematic approach Normal variants Periodontal staging

Extraoral Examination

Always examine extraoral first — observe the patient before they sit down. Note gait, posture, general wellbeing, facial asymmetry, skin changes.
General inspection & facial analysis ▾

Facial thirds: Hairline → Glabella / Glabella → Subnasale / Subnasale → Menton (should be roughly equal).

Facial fifths: Width of face divided into 5 equal parts, each equalling one eye width. Basis for aesthetic facial analysis.

Skin: Pallor (anaemia), jaundice (liver disease), malar flush (mitral stenosis/SLE), erythema, telangectasia, pigmentation changes (Addison's).

Asymmetry causes: Parotid swelling, masseteric hypertrophy (bruxism), unilateral condylar hyperplasia, bony swellings (fibro-osseous lesions, Paget's), scarring, facial palsy.

Lymph node examination — full sequence ▾

Palpation sequence: Submental → Submandibular → Pre-auricular → Post-auricular → Superficial cervical → Deep cervical chain → Posterior triangle → Supraclavicular.

Assess for each node: Size (>1cm = significant), consistency (soft/firm/rubbery/hard), mobility (fixed = strongly suggests malignancy), tenderness (infection = tender), number of nodes, bilaterality.

Interpretation:

  • Hard, fixed, non-tender, irregular → suspect metastatic malignancy (oral SCC, thyroid, lymphoma)
  • Rubbery, discrete, non-tender → lymphoma
  • Soft, tender, mobile → reactive (infection, viral illness)
TMJ assessment ▾

Palpation: Place index fingers immediately anterior to tragus of ear (lateral poles of condyles). Ask patient to open and close. Feel for: condylar movement, clicking (single = disc displacement with reduction; multiple = disc adhesion), crepitus (degenerative joint disease).

Maximum mouth opening: Normal 35–55mm (upper to lower incisors). <35mm = restricted (causes: trismus, disc displacement without reduction, fibrosis, ankylosis).

Deviation on opening: Early deviation that corrects = disc displacement with reduction. Persistent deviation = muscle imbalance or ankylosis.

Lateral excursions: Normal 8–12mm each side. Pain on lateral movement = joint or muscle pathology.

Salivary gland examination ▾

Parotid: Inspect (swelling below/in front of ear lobe = parotid territory). Bimanual palpation — finger intraorally near Stensen's duct orifice (opposite upper 6), hand externally. Massage gland to assess salivary flow — should produce clear saliva.

Submandibular: Bimanual — index finger on floor of mouth, hand under chin. Wharton's duct opens near the lingual frenulum (caruncle). Normal gland feels like a firm oval lobulated mass under the mandible.

Sublingual: Visible as bilateral ridges (plica sublingualis) on the floor of the mouth.

Assess: Secretion quality (clear = normal, cloudy = sialadenitis, purulent = abscess, blood-stained = duct neoplasm), tenderness, calculi (bimanual along duct track).

Always palpate lymph nodes bimanually and follow the systematic sequence. A missed malignant node can delay diagnosis and be a serious clinical error.

Intraoral Examination

Examine soft tissues BEFORE teeth. Use good lighting, mouth mirror, and gauze to move tongue. Follow a systematic route so nothing is missed.
Systematic soft tissue examination route ▾

Standard route: Lips (vermilion border + mucosal surface) → Labial mucosa (upper & lower) → Commissures → Buccal mucosa (L + R, check opposite upper 6 for parotid papilla) → Upper alveolus/gingivae → Lower alveolus/gingivae → Hard palate (incisive papilla, palatal rugae, torus) → Soft palate & uvula → Oropharynx → Tongue dorsum (papillae, fissures, geographic patches) → Lateral borders of tongue (retract with gauze — HIGHEST RISK SITE) → Ventral tongue → Floor of mouth (lift tongue — SECOND HIGHEST RISK SITE) → Salivary duct orifices → Gingival margins.

Lateral border of tongue + floor of mouth = highest risk sites for oral SCC. Never skip. Bimanual palpation of floor of mouth.

Lesion description — structured approach ▾

Describe using: Type, Site, Size, Shape, Colour, Surface, Border, Base, Consistency, Number, Symptoms, Duration.

Lesion types: Macule (flat colour change), Patch (macule >1cm), Papule (raised <5mm), Plaque (raised >5mm), Vesicle (fluid-filled <5mm), Bulla (>5mm), Pustule (pus-filled), Erosion (superficial epithelial loss), Ulcer (full-thickness loss), Nodule (firm raised), Fissure (linear crack).

Border: Well-defined vs diffuse; raised vs flat; rolled (everted = malignancy sign) vs regular.

Base: Indurated (hard = malignancy sign) vs soft.

Palpation: Assess for induration, fixation to underlying tissue, fluctuance (abscess/cyst). Always palpate bimanually for floor of mouth/tongue lesions.

Pulpal & periapical tests ▾

Cold test (Endo-Ice / CO₂ snow): Positive (pain during stimulus, stops when removed) = vital pulp. Pain lingering >30 seconds = irreversible pulpitis. No response = pulp necrosis or heavily restored tooth.

Electric pulp test (EPT): Tests neural viability. Positive (sensation) = vital. Not reliable in recently traumatised teeth or mature apices in children.

Heat test (GP stick): Reproduces symptoms of irreversible pulpitis — pain lingers after heat removed.

Percussion (vertical): Tenderness = periapical inflammation/abscess. Always compare with adjacent teeth.

Percussion (horizontal): Tenderness = lateral periodontal involvement.

Palpation over apex (buccal): Tenderness = periapical pathology. Buccal sinus = chronic periapical abscess.

Transillumination: Identifies cracks in teeth (cracked tooth syndrome).

Test cavity: Last resort when all other tests inconclusive — no LA, drill into dentine — sensation = vital.

No single test is diagnostic. Always correlate pulp tests with radiographic findings, history, and clinical signs before reaching a diagnosis.

2018 AAP/EFP Classification — Staging & Grading ▾

Staging (severity and complexity):

  • Stage I (mild): CAL 1–2mm, bone loss coronal 1/3, no tooth loss due to periodontitis, PPD ≤4mm
  • Stage II (moderate): CAL 3–4mm, bone loss coronal 1/3, no tooth loss due to perio, PPD ≤5mm
  • Stage III (severe, potential tooth loss): CAL ≥5mm, bone loss extending to mid or apical 1/3, tooth loss ≤4 due to perio, PPD ≥6mm, furcation Class II/III, mobility Grade II/III
  • Stage IV (severe, masticatory dysfunction): As Stage III + masticatory dysfunction (bite collapse, flaring, drifting, ≤20 remaining teeth)

Grading (rate of progression, risk factors):

  • Grade A (slow): No evidence of progression, bone loss/age <0.25, non-smoker, normoglycaemic
  • Grade B (moderate): <2mm bone loss over 5 years, bone loss/age 0.25–1.0, smoker <10 cigs/day, HbA1c <7%
  • Grade C (rapid): ≥2mm bone loss over 5 years, bone loss/age >1.0, smoker ≥10/day, HbA1c ≥7%, or risk factors: immunosuppression

🩺 Oral Lesions

Systematic classification and clinical management of white, red, ulcerative, vesicular, and pigmented oral lesions. Duration, location, and morphology are diagnostic keys.

Malignant potential 3-week rule Biopsy criteria

White Lesions

Cannot be wiped off (except pseudomembranous candida)
Leukoplakia — definition & management ▾

Definition: White patch that cannot be wiped off and cannot be clinically or pathologically characterised as any other known disease — an exclusion diagnosis.

Types:

  • Homogeneous: Flat, uniform white colour. Smooth or wrinkled. Lower malignant transformation rate (~1–2%).
  • Non-homogeneous: Speckled (erythroleukoplakia — red and white mixed), nodular, verrucous. Higher malignant transformation rate (up to 33% for erythroleukoplakia).

Overall malignant transformation: 1–5% over 10 years, but up to 33% for erythroleukoplakia.

High-risk sites: Floor of mouth, ventrolateral tongue, soft palate complex.

Management: (1) Risk factor elimination (smoking, alcohol, betel nut). (2) Review after 2–4 weeks. (3) Biopsy all non-homogeneous lesions and any persistent homogeneous lesions. (4) Excision if dysplasia present. (5) Long-term follow-up mandatory — annual review minimum.

Oral candidosis — all types ▾

Pseudomembranous (thrush): White/cream plaques that WIPE OFF to reveal raw red/bleeding base. Found in newborns, immunocompromised, denture wearers, after antibiotics.

Erythematous (atrophic): Red patches on palate (often mirror denture shape), dorsal tongue (median rhomboid glossitis pattern). Common with inhalers (steroid) — advise to rinse after use.

Hyperplastic (candidal leukoplakia): White plaque that does NOT wipe off. Resistant to treatment. Pre-malignant potential. Biopsy required.

Angular cheilitis: Fissuring of commissures. Often mixed infection: Candida + Staphylococcus aureus. Associated with vitamin deficiencies, iron deficiency, denture over-closure.

Diagnosis: Clinical appearance ± smear (PAS stain shows hyphae) ± culture. Consider underlying immunosuppression if recurrent or severe (HIV, diabetes, leukaemia).

Treatment: Topical: Nystatin suspension 100,000 units/mL QDS held in mouth. Systemic: Fluconazole 50–150mg OD × 7–14 days. Treat dentures (soak in dilute Milton, replace after 6 weeks). Correct predisposing factors.

Lichen planus — types, pathology, management ▾

Types:

  • Reticular (most common): Interlacing white lines (Wickham's striae). Bilateral buccal mucosa. Usually asymptomatic.
  • Erosive/Ulcerative: Most clinically significant. Painful central erosions surrounded by white striae. May be severe — interferes with eating.
  • Atrophic: Red patches with surrounding striae. Common on gingiva (desquamative gingivitis).
  • Plaque type: Homogeneous white plaque on tongue dorsum. Looks like leukoplakia — biopsy to distinguish.
  • Bullous: Rare. Large fluid-filled blisters that rupture to leave erosions.

Sites: Bilateral buccal mucosa most common. Also tongue, gingiva (desquamative gingivitis = patchy peeling red gingiva), lips, palate.

Pathology: T-cell-mediated chronic inflammatory condition. Histology: Band-like lymphocytic infiltrate at epithelium–connective tissue interface, saw-tooth rete processes, hydropic degeneration of basal cell layer, colloid (Civatte) bodies.

Management: Topical corticosteroids (Beclomethasone spray, Clobetasol propionate 0.05% gel — most potent). Tacrolimus ointment (calcineurin inhibitor) for corticosteroid-resistant cases. Systemic prednisolone for severe flares. Annual review — 1% malignant transformation risk.

Lichenoid reactions vs lichen planus ▾

Clinically and histologically identical to LP but have an identifiable causative agent.

Common causes: NSAIDs, antihypertensives (beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, methyldopa), antimalarials (hydroxychloroquine), amalgam restorations (contact reaction — lesion adjacent to restoration), gold therapy.

Management: Remove causative agent → often resolves completely. If amalgam-related and lesion is adjacent, replace with composite/ceramic. If not resolving, treat as LP.

White sponge naevus ▾

Autosomal dominant condition (mutation in Keratin 4 or Keratin 13 genes).

Diffuse, white, spongy, folded/wrinkled mucosa — bilateral and often extensive, involving buccal mucosa, floor of mouth, alveolar mucosa. Present from birth or early childhood. May also affect oesophagus, nasal and genital mucosa.

Key: Family history is important. Completely benign — no malignant potential. No treatment needed. Reassure patient and family.

High-risk white lesions requiring biopsy: Non-homogeneous leukoplakia (especially speckled/erythroleukoplakia), any leukoplakia on floor of mouth or lateral tongue, persistent despite risk factor elimination, any induration or fixation to deep tissue, associated lymphadenopathy.

Red Lesions

Erythroplakia: highest malignant potential of all oral lesions
Erythroplakia — must know ▾

Definition: Bright red, velvety patch of oral mucosa that cannot be attributed to any other clinically or histopathologically definable condition.

Malignant transformation: 40–50% — far higher than leukoplakia. This is the most dangerous oral potentially malignant disorder.

Appearance: Velvety, bright red, well-demarcated, often flat or slightly depressed. May be mixed with white areas (erythroleukoplakia).

Management: Biopsy immediately. Histology typically shows severe dysplasia or carcinoma in situ. Excision with clear margins. Long-term surveillance essential.

Median rhomboid glossitis ▾

Rhomboid or oval erythematous area on the midline dorsal tongue, anterior to the circumvallate papillae. The area is depapillated — smooth and red.

Historically considered a developmental anomaly (persistence of the tuberculum impar). Now understood to be predominantly associated with Candida (erythematous type).

Common in smokers, denture wearers. A "kissing lesion" may appear on the opposing palate (erythematous patch on hard palate corresponding to tongue contact).

Management: Antifungal treatment (fluconazole 50mg OD × 7–14 days). Biopsy if non-responsive.

Vascular lesions — diascopy ▾

Diascopy: Press a glass slide or clear instrument against the lesion. If it blanches (becomes pale) → vascular lesion (filled with blood that can be displaced). If it does NOT blanch → haemorrhage/petechiae (blood is outside vessels).

Haemangioma: Congenital vascular malformation. Blanches. Often bluish-red. May be capillary or cavernous. May involute in childhood. Refer before any surgical intervention to assess type.

Port wine stain (Sturge-Weber): Unilateral, follows trigeminal nerve distribution (usually V2/V3). Associated with: ipsilateral choroidal haemangioma (→ glaucoma), leptomeningeal angioma (→ epilepsy, intellectual disability). Oral: gingival hyperplasia, accelerated eruption on affected side.

Petechiae and ecchymoses: Do NOT blanch. Causes: trauma, thrombocytopenia, vasculitis, anticoagulants, infectious mononucleosis (palatal petechiae).

Ulcerative Lesions

Duration is the critical variable
Recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS) — full classification ▾

Minor RAS (80%): <10mm, 1–5 ulcers, round/oval with grey pseudomembrane and erythematous halo. ONLY non-keratinised mucosa (buccal, labial, floor of mouth, soft palate). Heal in 7–14 days without scarring.

Major RAS (Sutton's disease, 10%): >10mm diameter, 1–3 ulcers, last weeks–months, ANY oral site including keratinised mucosa, heal with scarring. May mimic carcinoma.

Herpetiform RAS (10%): 1–3mm, 10–100 small ulcers simultaneously. Coalesce to form larger irregular ulcers. ANY oral site. Heal in 2 weeks. Despite name, NOT caused by herpes virus.

Triggers: Stress, trauma, SLS (sodium lauryl sulphate — switch SLS-free toothpaste), foods (tomatoes, chocolate, nuts, cheese), hormonal changes (premenstrual), cessation of smoking (paradoxically improves with smoking but worsens on stopping).

Investigations to rule out: Iron deficiency (ferritin), vitamin B12, folate deficiency, coeliac disease (anti-tTG antibodies), Crohn's disease, Behçet's syndrome (if concurrent genital ulcers + eye disease + positive pathergy test).

Treatment: Topical corticosteroids (triamcinolone paste, beclomethasone inhaler applied), tetracycline 250mg mouthwash (held 3 min QDS × 3 days), amlexanox paste. Severe: systemic prednisolone, colchicine, dapsone, thalidomide.

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) ▾

Sites: Lateral and ventral tongue (most common), floor of mouth, retromolar pad, soft palate complex. Lower lip (squamous cell carcinoma from actinic damage).

Appearance: Ulcer with indurated (hard), raised, rolled (everted) edges. Red or mixed red-white base. Fixed to underlying tissue. Associated cervical lymphadenopathy. Often painless in early stages — a key feature (pain is a late feature).

Risk factors: Tobacco (all forms) + alcohol = synergistic (risk ×30). HPV-16 (oropharyngeal SCC — younger non-smokers). Areca nut/betel quid. Previous oral SCC. Immunosuppression. Prior radiation. Iron deficiency (Paterson-Kelly syndrome).

Red flags (unexplained — must refer urgently): Non-healing ulcer >3 weeks, unexplained lump in neck, unexplained persistent loose tooth, unexplained numbness of lip/chin (IAN infiltration), dysphagia, trismus (pterygoid infiltration), white/red patch.

Management: Urgent 2-week wait referral to OMFS/oral medicine. Incisional biopsy. TNM staging (clinical + imaging: CT/MRI/PET-CT). MDT discussion. Treatment: surgery ± radiotherapy ± chemotherapy (depending on stage and site).

Necrotising ulcerative gingivitis (NUG/ANUG) ▾

Classic triad: (1) Interdental papilla necrosis — "punched-out" or "cratered" papillae with grey pseudomembrane. (2) Pain — severe and spontaneous. (3) Bleeding — spontaneous or with minimal provocation.

Additional features: Fetid halitosis (characteristic — fusobacteria), grey pseudomembrane, fever and malaise (systemic involvement), cervical lymphadenopathy.

Microbiology: Polymicrobial fusospirochaetal infection — Treponema denticola, Prevotella intermedia, Fusobacterium nucleatum.

Risk factors: Psychological stress, smoking, poor oral hygiene, immunocompromise. If severe or recurring → consider HIV testing.

Treatment: Immediate: gentle debridement (supragingival only initially), chlorhexidine 0.2% mouthwash, metronidazole 400mg TDS × 5 days (excellent anaerobic spectrum), smoking cessation. Once acute phase resolved: definitive periodontal treatment to restore papillae where possible.

Progression: NUG → NUP (necrotising ulcerative periodontitis — extends to bone) → Noma (cancrum oris — in severely malnourished/immunocompromised — rare in developed world).

The 3-week rule: Any ulcer persisting beyond 3 weeks without an obvious, confirmed benign cause requires either biopsy or urgent specialist referral. Do not repeatedly reassure and review without histological diagnosis.

Vesicular & Bullous Lesions

Viral vs autoimmune — histology & DIF required
Primary herpetic gingivostomatitis ▾

Cause: Primary infection with HSV-1. Common in children 1–5 years and young adults.

Prodrome (1–2 days): Malaise, fever, headache, tender cervical lymphadenopathy.

Oral features: Multiple small vesicles → rapidly burst → shallow painful ulcers with erythematous halo. Distribution: BOTH keratinised (gingiva, palate, dorsal tongue) AND non-keratinised mucosa — affects ANY oral site. Diffuse acute marginal gingivitis (distinctive feature). Highly contagious — no sharing utensils/kissing.

Healing: 1–2 weeks spontaneously.

Treatment: Supportive — adequate fluids (risk of dehydration in children), soft diet, analgesics (paracetamol/ibuprofen), chlorhexidine 0.12–0.2% mouthwash. Antiviral: Aciclovir 200mg 5×/day × 5 days (or 100mg 5×/day under 2 years) — only effective if started within 72 hours of onset. Reserve for severe cases/immunocompromised.

Herpes labialis (recurrent herpes) ▾

Reactivation of latent HSV-1 in trigeminal ganglion. Reactivated virus travels down nerve to produce lesions at vermilion border and perioral skin.

Prodrome: Tingling, burning, itching at affected site (4–48h before lesion appears).

Progression: Erythema → papule → vesicle cluster → ulceration/erosion → crusting → healing (7–10 days).

Triggers: UV light (sunburn), stress, illness (fever — "cold sore"), menstruation, trauma, immunosuppression.

Treatment: Topical aciclovir 5% cream — applied at PRODROME stage (before vesicles form) 5×/day × 5 days. Systemic valaciclovir/aciclovir for severe or immunocompromised patients. Sun protection (SPF lip balm) reduces UV-triggered recurrences.

Herpes zoster — trigeminal branch ▾

Reactivation of varicella zoster virus (VZV) from dormancy in cranial nerve ganglia.

Features: Unilateral distribution along one trigeminal branch (V2 or V3 most common). Severe, burning pain precedes rash by several days (may mimic toothache — critical diagnostic awareness needed). Skin vesicles/crusting along dermatome — does NOT cross midline. Intraoral vesicles and ulceration on ipsilateral side.

Complications: Post-herpetic neuralgia (PHN) — persistent pain for months/years after healing, especially in elderly. V1 (ophthalmic) involvement = Herpes zoster ophthalmicus → risk of blindness. Hutchinson's sign: vesicles on tip of nose (nasociliary branch V1) = eye involvement imminent.

Treatment: Aciclovir 800mg 5×/day × 7 days — must start within 72h of rash onset to reduce PHN risk. Gabapentin/pregabalin for PHN. Steroids controversial.

Pemphigus vulgaris vs mucous membrane pemphigoid (MMP) ▾

Pemphigus vulgaris (PV):

  • Autoimmune: IgG autoantibodies against desmogleins (Dsg3 → oral only; Dsg3 + Dsg1 → oral + skin)
  • Intraepithelial split (acantholysis) → fragile, easily ruptured blisters → extensive painful erosions
  • Nikolsky sign POSITIVE — lateral pressure on apparently normal mucosa induces blister
  • Oral lesions often the FIRST manifestation (months before skin lesions)
  • Life-threatening without treatment (pre-steroid era 100% mortality)
  • DIF: IgG in chicken-wire (intercellular) pattern throughout epithelium
  • Treatment: High-dose systemic prednisolone (1mg/kg/day) + steroid-sparing agent (azathioprine, mycophenolate, rituximab)

Mucous membrane pemphigoid (MMP):

  • Autoimmune: IgG/IgA against basement membrane zone proteins (BP180, BP230, laminin 332)
  • Subepithelial split → firmer, less fragile blisters, desquamative gingivitis most common oral presentation
  • Nikolsky sign may be positive but less consistently
  • Ocular involvement (30%): Progressive scarring → fornix obliteration → symblepharon → blindness. ALL patients with MMP must have urgent ophthalmology referral.

Radiological Investigations

ALARA principle: As Low As Reasonably Achievable. Justify (clinical need), Optimise (lowest dose for diagnostic quality), Limit (to only what's needed). Follow IR(ME)R regulations.
Periapical radiograph ▾

Indications: Periapical pathology assessment, root morphology, periodontal bone levels, post-operative endo check, trauma assessment, implant planning (limited).

Paralleling technique (preferred): Film/sensor parallel to tooth, long cone perpendicular to both. Less geometric distortion, reproducible, better image quality.

Bisecting angle technique: More distortion, used when paralleling is impossible. Prone to elongation or foreshortening errors.

Limitations: 2D image only. Facial/palatal cortical plates not visible. Early periapical changes not evident until >30% bone mineral loss. Cannot show buccal/lingual extent of lesions.

Bitewing radiograph — caries detection ▾

Gold standard for: Interproximal caries detection, secondary caries under restorations, alveolar crest bone level assessment.

Recall frequency (FGDP guidelines): High caries risk: 6-monthly. Medium risk: 12-monthly. Low risk: 24-monthly (adults), 12-monthly (children).

Radiographic caries classification:

  • E1: Caries in outer enamel (demineralisation) — monitor, fluoride
  • E2: Caries in inner enamel approaching DEJ — fissure sealant consideration
  • D1: Outer 1/3 dentine — generally restore
  • D2: Middle 1/3 dentine — restore with consideration of pulpal proximity
  • D3: Inner 1/3 dentine near pulp — high risk of irreversible pulpitis; consider pulp therapy

Radiography underestimates caries extent — always correlate with clinical findings. ICDAS system for clinical caries recording.

Dental panoramic tomogram (DPT/OPG) ▾

Indications: Comprehensive survey of all teeth, assessment of third molars, developing dentition, large lesions, fractures, TMJ assessment, sinus pathology, pathology screening in new patients.

Key limitations: Significant geometric distortion (magnification ~25%), superimposition of structures, ghosts of opposing jaw structures, poor interproximal caries detection, patient positioning critical (chin position affects image).

Report systematically: Start with teeth → alveolar bone → condyles/TMJ → rami and angles → mandibular body → maxillary sinuses (Schneiderian membrane thickening, opacification, polyps) → nasal cavity → soft tissues. Never miss an incidental finding.

CBCT — indications & radiation dose ▾

Justified indications: Implant planning (bone volume, density, anatomy), assessment of impacted teeth (3rd molar relationship to IAN, palatally impacted canines), complex endodontics (extra canals, root resorption, perforation, failed endo assessment), orthognathic surgery planning, jaw pathology extent and cortical involvement.

NOT indicated for: Routine dental caries, routine periodontal assessment — radiation dose too high for information gained.

Radiation dose comparison: Small FOV CBCT ≈ 10× OPG. Large FOV CBCT ≈ 50× OPG. Medical CT ≈ 100× OPG.

SEDENTEXCT guideline: CBCT only when: conventional radiography and clinical examination cannot provide sufficient diagnostic information AND the information will change patient management.

Consider MRI instead: For soft tissue assessment (tumour extent, TMJ disc morphology, salivary gland pathology) — no ionising radiation.

For third molar assessment: only take CBCT when the OPG shows 1 or more of the Hunter-Greer signs indicating close IAN relationship (interruption of white lines of canal, darkening of root, deflection of root, narrowing of root, narrowing/diversion of canal).

Biopsy Techniques

Gold standard for oral mucosal diagnosis
Incisional biopsy — technique & site selection ▾

Indication: Large lesion where full excision is not practical, lesion appears clinically suspicious for malignancy, or when diagnosis must be confirmed before definitive treatment.

Site selection (critical): Sample the most representative/abnormal area. Include red or speckled areas (higher grade lesion). Avoid necrotic/ulcerated centre (non-diagnostic — only necrotic debris). Include the junction of lesion and adjacent normal-appearing tissue to show pathological changes at the margins.

Technique: LA injected AWAY from biopsy site (to avoid distortion). Elliptical incision (fusiform). Minimum dimensions: 5mm × 10mm, adequate depth into submucosa (full-thickness epithelium + connective tissue needed). Minimal use of diathermy/cautery at margins (causes artefact). Handle specimen gently — do not crush with forceps. Resorbable sutures to close. Label pot with formol saline. Accurate clinical details and description on request form.

Excisional biopsy ▾

Indication: Small (<1cm) lesion where complete removal is both diagnostic AND therapeutic. Suitable for clinically benign lesions (fibrous epulis, mucocele, small leukoplakia, melanotic macule) or small well-defined lesions unlikely to be malignant.

Margins: 2–3mm of clinically normal tissue for benign lesion. 5–10mm if malignancy suspected (but incisional biopsy preferred for suspected malignancy first).

Never excise a suspected major salivary gland pleomorphic adenoma. Inadequate margins → tumour spillage → recurrence. These need formal parotidectomy/submandibular gland excision with appropriate margins.

Direct immunofluorescence (DIF) — vesiculobullous disease ▾

Essential for: Pemphigus vulgaris, mucous membrane pemphigoid, epidermolysis bullosa acquisita, linear IgA disease, dermatitis herpetiformis.

CRITICAL: Biopsy perilesional apparently normal mucosa — NOT the bulla itself (epithelium already destroyed, no diagnostic information). Take 2–3mm from the edge of a recent lesion.

Fixative: Michel's medium — NOT formalin. Formalin destroys immunoglobulins required for DIF. Michel's medium preserves immunoglobulins. Label separately from routine biopsy pot.

Laboratory process: Snap-frozen sections cut. Fluorescent-labelled antibodies applied for IgG, IgA, IgM, and complement (C3).

Results:

  • PV: Intercellular IgG (chicken-wire pattern throughout epithelium)
  • MMP: Linear IgG (± IgA, C3) at basement membrane zone
  • Linear IgA disease: Linear IgA at BMZ
  • Dermatitis herpetiformis: Granular IgA in dermal papillae
Histological reporting — epithelial dysplasia ▾

Dysplasia features: nuclear hyperchromatism, pleomorphism, increased nuclear-cytoplasmic ratio, abnormal mitotic figures (especially suprabasal), loss of cellular polarity, individual cell dyskeratosis (premature keratinisation), loss of epithelial stratification, drop-shaped rete processes.

Grading:

  • Mild dysplasia: Changes confined to basal 1/3 of epithelium
  • Moderate dysplasia: Changes in basal 2/3 of epithelium
  • Severe dysplasia / CIS: Changes in all layers (but basement membrane intact)
  • Carcinoma in situ: Full-thickness dysplastic changes, basement membrane intact
  • Invasive SCC: Malignant cells have breached basement membrane
Before aspirating any lesion: Check if it blanches on diascopy. If it blanches → vascular lesion → do NOT aspirate. Refer to specialist. Aspiration of haemangioma can be catastrophic.

Microbiology & Salivary Investigations

Microbiological investigations ▾

Swab: Surface exudate from lesions. Culture and sensitivity — identifies organisms and antibiotic sensitivity. Useful for bacterial infections, candida (culture on Sabouraud's agar).

Smear/cytology: PAS stain for Candida hyphae (pseudohyphae indicate invasive candida). Tzanck smear (HSV) shows multinucleated giant cells with intranuclear inclusions.

Pus culture: Aspirated from deep abscess for culture and sensitivities — guides antibiotic choice for refractory infections.

PCR: High sensitivity and specificity. HSV typing, HPV genotyping (high-risk strains 16/18 for oropharyngeal SCC), Mycobacterium tuberculosis (TB causing cervical lymphadenopathy), EBV.

Serology: HIV (p24 antigen + antibody combined test), hepatitis B (HBsAg, anti-HBs, anti-HBc), hepatitis C (anti-HCV), syphilis (VDRL/RPR + TPHA/FTA-ABS).

Salivary gland investigations ▾

Salivary flow rate: Stimulated whole saliva <0.1 mL/min = hyposalivation (clinical xerostomia). Unstimulated whole saliva <0.1 mL/min over 15 minutes.

Ultrasound: First-line for salivary gland swelling. Identifies calculi (hyperechoic with shadowing), cysts, tumour vascularity, lymph node assessment. No radiation.

Sialography: Contrast medium injected into duct orifice → fluoroscopy or plain film. Identifies: calculi (filling defects), strictures, duct dilatation, sialectasis (dewdrop appearance in Sjögren's). Now largely replaced by MRI sialography (no radiation, no contrast injection).

Minor salivary gland biopsy (lip): For Sjögren's syndrome confirmation. Incise inner lower lip mucosa, harvest ≥5 gland lobules. Diagnostic: focal lymphocytic sialadenitis with ≥1 focus (cluster ≥50 lymphocytes) per 4mm² tissue area.

Scintigraphy (technetium-99m pertechnetate): Functional assessment of all salivary glands simultaneously. Decreased uptake and delayed secretion in Sjögren's. Rarely used now.

Sjögren's syndrome — diagnostic tests ▾

Anti-Ro (SS-A) and Anti-La (SS-B) antibodies: Most specific serological markers for primary Sjögren's syndrome. Anti-Ro more sensitive.

ANA (antinuclear antibodies): Positive in ~70–80% of Sjögren's patients. Non-specific.

RF (rheumatoid factor): Often positive (especially secondary Sjögren's with RA).

Schirmer's test: Filter paper strips placed under lower eyelid for 5 minutes. <5mm wetting = reduced lacrimal secretion (positive for ocular component).

2016 ACR/EULAR Classification Criteria (≥4 points = primary Sjögren's):

  • Lip biopsy with focal lymphocytic sialadenitis: 3 points
  • Anti-Ro/SSA positive: 3 points
  • Ocular staining score ≥5: 1 point
  • Schirmer's ≤5mm/5min: 1 point
  • Unstimulated salivary flow ≤0.1mL/min: 1 point

Local Anaesthetics

Mechanism, dosing & toxicity
Mechanism of action — why LA fails in infection ▾

LAs are weak bases (pKa 7.7–8.9). They exist in both ionised (BH⁺) and unionised (B) forms in equilibrium. Only the unionised (lipid-soluble) form can cross the nerve membrane. Once inside the axon, the ionised form blocks the voltage-gated Na⁺ channel from the intracellular side.

Why LA fails in infected tissue: Infected tissue has lower pH (acidic environment → more H⁺ ions). Henderson-Hasselbalch: at lower pH, equilibrium shifts toward ionised form (BH⁺). Less unionised drug available to cross nerve membrane → significantly reduced efficacy. This explains why achieving adequate LA in a "hot" pulp or acute abscess is clinically challenging.

Solutions: Increase dose, buffered LA (sodium bicarbonate — raises pH, more unionised), give time for acute inflammation to subside, use supplemental techniques (intraosseous, intrapulpal, PDL injection).

Adrenaline content & cardiovascular limits ▾

1:80,000 = 12.5 μg/mL. 1:100,000 = 10 μg/mL. 1:200,000 = 5 μg/mL.

Adrenaline benefits in dental LA: Reduces systemic absorption → lower peak plasma levels → safer. Increases duration of anaesthesia. Reduces bleeding in surgical field. Haemostasis. The benefits of low-dose vasoconstrictor generally outweigh risks even in most cardiovascular patients.

Max dose calculation example ▾

Example: 70kg patient, Lidocaine 2% with 1:80,000 adrenaline.

Max dose = 4.4 mg/kg × 70kg = 308mg lidocaine.

Each cartridge (2.2mL) of 2% lidocaine = 2.2 × 20mg/mL = 44mg.

Number of cartridges = 308mg ÷ 44mg = 7 cartridges maximum (limited by adrenaline at 0.2mg = ~8 cartridges, so lidocaine is limiting factor).

Bisphosphonates & MRONJ ▾

Drugs: Oral: Alendronate (weekly), risedronate. IV: Zoledronic acid (monthly/yearly), pamidronate, ibandronate. Also: Denosumab (anti-RANK-L monoclonal antibody — similar risk).

Mechanism: Inhibit osteoclast activity → reduced bone remodelling → risk of non-healing necrosis when dento-alveolar surgery creates wound that requires remodelling to heal.

MRONJ risk: Oral bisphosphonates: ~0.1% (osteoporosis indication). IV bisphosphonates for cancer: 1–10%. Denosumab: similar to IV bisphosphonates.

MRONJ definition (AAOMS 2014): Current or previous treatment with anti-resorptive or anti-angiogenic agent + exposed necrotic bone (or probing through fistula to bone) in the maxillofacial region + persistent for >8 weeks + no history of radiation therapy to jaws.

Before starting bisphosphonate therapy: Comprehensive dental exam. Extract failing teeth. Complete any necessary surgical procedures. Optimise oral hygiene. Educate patient.

During bisphosphonate therapy: Avoid elective dento-alveolar surgery if possible. If extraction necessary: inform patient of risk, conservative approach (minimal bone removal, primary wound closure), avoid periosteal stripping, consider antibiotic cover, no healing by secondary intention. NEVER stop IV bisphosphonate without oncologist discussion.

MRONJ management: Conservative (antimicrobial mouthwash, antibiotics, pain control, sequestrum removal when loose), surgical debridement for refractory cases, PRGF (growth factors), hyperbaric oxygen (controversial).

Anticoagulants & antiplatelets — dental management ▾

Warfarin: Check INR on day of procedure or within 72h. INR <4.0 → proceed with local haemostatic measures (oxidised cellulose/Surgicel, resorbable sutures, 4.8% tranexamic acid mouthwash × 5 days, pressure). Do NOT routinely stop warfarin — thromboembolism risk from stopping outweighs manageable dental bleeding risk. INR >4.0 → postpone, contact prescribing physician.

DOACs (dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban): For minor dental surgery — generally do NOT interrupt. Perform procedure at trough level (12–24h after last dose for twice-daily agents). No reliable routine monitoring. For major surgery: discuss with physician (hold for 24–48h for dabigatran, 24h for others, longer if CKD).

Aspirin/clopidogrel: Do NOT stop — risk of acute cardiovascular/cerebrovascular event far outweighs dental bleeding risk. Manage bleeding with local haemostasis. Dual antiplatelet (aspirin + clopidogrel) — higher bleeding risk: consider hospital setting for complex extractions, consult cardiologist if considering interruption.

Local haemostatic measures: Oxidised cellulose (Surgicel/Surgical), absorbable gelatin sponge (Gelfoam), resorbable sutures, direct pressure (30 min minimum), tranexamic acid mouthwash (10mg/kg IV or 4.8% mouthwash QDS × 5 days), local LA with vasoconstrictor, careful surgical technique.

Important drug-induced oral effects (complete list) ▾

Gingival hyperplasia (overgrowth): Phenytoin (epilepsy), Cyclosporin (immunosuppressant), Nifedipine and other calcium channel blockers (amlodipine, diltiazem). Mechanism: altered fibroblast function and reduced collagen degradation. Exacerbated by poor plaque control. Management: improve OH, consider drug substitution (discuss with physician), surgical gingivectomy if severe.

Xerostomia (>500 drugs!): Antidepressants (TCA, SSRI, SNRI), antihistamines (H1 blockers), antipsychotics, antihypertensives (beta-blockers, diuretics, clonidine), antimuscarinics (bladder drugs — oxybutynin), opioids, antiepileptics (carbamazepine), systemic retinoids.

Oral ulceration: NSAIDs (aspirin-related mucosal damage), methotrexate (folate antagonism), Nicorandil (large non-healing ulcers — classic association), captopril/ACE inhibitors, gold, alendronate (oesophageal).

Oral candidiasis: Inhaled corticosteroids (advise spacer device + rinsing mouth after use), systemic corticosteroids, broad-spectrum antibiotics, immunosuppressants (cyclosporin, azathioprine), chemotherapy.

Lichenoid reactions: NSAIDs, beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, antimalarials (hydroxychloroquine — commonly used in RA/SLE), gold, penicillamine, methyldopa.

Altered taste/dysgeusia: ACE inhibitors (metallic taste — captopril classic), metronidazole, penicillamine, chemotherapy, carbamazepine, amiodarone.

Erythema multiforme: Sulphonamides, penicillin, phenytoin, barbiturates (also infections: HSV — most common trigger).

Black hairy tongue: Broad-spectrum antibiotics (disrupts oral flora), bismuth preparations, smoking, poor OH.

Sialadenosis (bilateral parotid enlargement): Antihypertensives (clonidine, methyldopa), phenylbutazone, iodine-containing drugs, thiouracil.

Chlorhexidine — comprehensive guide ▾

Mechanism: Cationic bisbiguanide → disrupts bacterial cell membranes → bactericidal at high concentration, bacteriostatic at low. Substantivity: Binds to oral surfaces and releases slowly over up to 12 hours — unique property responsible for prolonged antimicrobial effect.

Spectrum: Broad: Gram+, Gram−, fungi (Candida), some viruses (including HSV, HBV). Less effective against acid-fast bacteria, bacterial spores.

Formulations: 0.2% mouthwash (UK standard, 10mL BD); 0.12% (US); 1% gel (direct application to sockets, periodontal pockets); 0.05% for long-term use; chip/varnish (sub-gingival delivery — Perio Chip).

Uses: Post-surgical (implants, extractions — 48h post-op then mechanical OH can resume), NUG, aphthous ulcers adjunct, immunocompromised patients (when mechanical OH not possible), periodontal disease (adjunct to SRP).

Adverse effects: Brown staining of teeth, tongue, restorations (reversible with professional polish), altered/impaired taste perception, mucosal irritation (burning, desquamation) with prolonged use, bilateral parotid swelling (rare).

Incompatibilities: Anionic surfactants (SLS in toothpaste) inactivate CHX. Leave 30-minute gap between toothpaste use and CHX mouthwash. Not compatible with fluoride in same vehicle.

Cardiovascular Diseases

Hypertension — classification & dental management ▾

Classification (2018 ACC/AHA): Normal <120/80 → Elevated 120–129/<80 → Stage 1: 130–139/80–89 → Stage 2: ≥140/90 → Hypertensive crisis: >180/120.

Dental management:

  • Check BP at new patient appointment and before any procedure if concern
  • BP >180/110 (stage 3): defer elective dental care, refer urgently to GP. Emergency only in hospital setting.
  • Stress reduction: morning appointments (BP lowest), adequate anaesthesia (inadequate LA → pain → BP spike), anxiolysis (oral sedation), short appointments, good rapport
  • Adrenaline: safe to use with care. Limit to 2–3 cartridges of 1:100,000 in uncontrolled hypertension. Aspirate before injecting. Avoid gingival retraction cords soaked in adrenaline.
  • Drug interactions: NSAIDs reduce efficacy of antihypertensives and can cause sodium retention/BP increase (use cautiously). Epinephrine + non-selective beta-blocker (propranolol) → unopposed alpha stimulation → hypertensive episode + reflex bradycardia. Epinephrine + MAOIs → hypertensive crisis.

Oral effects of antihypertensive drugs: CCBs (nifedipine, amlodipine) → gingival hyperplasia. Diuretics → xerostomia. Beta-blockers → xerostomia, lichenoid reactions. ACE inhibitors → lichenoid reactions, angioedema (can be life-threatening — tongue/lip/throat swelling), altered taste.

Infective endocarditis — NICE vs AHA guidelines ▾

Causative organisms from dental procedures: Viridans group streptococci (S. sanguinis, S. oralis, S. mutans) — primary cause of IE from oral bacteraemia.

High-risk cardiac conditions for IE: Prosthetic heart valves (mechanical or bioprosthetic), previous episode of IE, congenital heart disease (unrepaired complex cyanotic, or repaired with residual defect), cardiac transplant with valvulopathy.

NICE 2008 (UK): Antibiotic prophylaxis for dental procedures is NOT recommended, even for high-risk patients. Evidence showed it was not cost-effective, potentially harmful, and of unproven benefit. However: maintain optimal oral health (most important). Prompt treatment of dental infections. Educate patients about IE symptoms.

AHA 2007 (US): Recommends amoxicillin 2g PO 30–60 min before procedure for high-risk patients. Penicillin allergy: clindamycin 600mg or azithromycin 500mg or cephalexin 2g.

Note: There is ongoing controversy between guidelines. Know both. In clinical practice: follow the guideline of your country. Regardless — optimal oral hygiene is paramount and prevents far more cases of IE than any antibiotic prophylaxis regimen.

Ischaemic heart disease — dental management ▾

Post-MI: Defer all elective dental treatment for minimum 3–6 months post-MI. Emergency only (analgesics, antibiotics, drainage), ideally in hospital setting. After 3 months: can treat in primary care with appropriate precautions.

Stable angina management: Ensure patient's GTN spray is available and accessible in surgery. Morning appointments (catecholamine peaks in morning — higher MI risk). Stress reduction protocol. Adequate anaesthesia is essential. Oxygen on standby.

Angina attack in chair: Stop treatment. Patient sits up (NOT supine — increases cardiac preload). GTN spray 400μg sublingually (1–2 puffs). Oxygen if available. If no relief in 5 minutes → second GTN dose → if still no relief → treat as MI → call 999 → aspirin 300mg (chewed) → oxygen → prepare AED.

Drug interactions: GTN (nitrates) + sildenafil (Viagra) → severe hypotension (absolute contraindication — ask about PDE5 inhibitors). Adrenaline: use with care but do not withhold — pain from inadequate anaesthesia is more dangerous. Limit to 2 cartridges of 1:100,000 in severe IHD.

Endocrine Diseases

Diabetes mellitus — oral manifestations & management ▾

Oral manifestations: Increased susceptibility to infections (bacterial, fungal, viral), impaired wound healing, xerostomia (reduced salivary flow), increased dental caries risk, parotid gland enlargement (bilateral), burning mouth sensation, dysgeusia, periodontal disease (accelerated and more severe — bidirectional relationship).

Perio-diabetes bidirectional relationship: Chronic hyperglycaemia → increased susceptibility to periodontal pathogens, impaired neutrophil function, increased AGEs (advanced glycation end products) → accelerated periodontal destruction. Conversely: treating periodontitis has been shown to improve HbA1c by approximately 0.5% — this is clinically significant.

Dental management:

  • Morning appointments (insulin action, food intake more predictable)
  • Confirm patient has eaten and taken usual medication before appointment
  • Have glucose/fruit juice/sugar available in surgery for hypoglycaemia
  • HbA1c <8.5% → routine dental treatment. HbA1c >10% → increased risk, discuss with physician, avoid elective major surgery if possible
  • Prescribe antibiotics more readily for infections (impaired immune response)
  • Avoid prolonged fasting (concern if patient on metformin → lactic acidosis risk with prolonged starvation)

Hypoglycaemia in the dental chair: Recognition — sudden pallor, sweating, trembling, confusion, unusual behaviour. Management: if conscious → oral glucose (Glucogel, Glucotabs, juice). If unconscious → IM glucagon 1mg (or IV glucose 20%/50%). Call 999. Do NOT continue dental treatment.

Thyroid disorders ▾

Hypothyroidism: Macroglossia (enlarged tongue — may affect airways), delayed tooth eruption, slowed healing, perioral oedema, hoarse voice, dry skin and hair. Anaemia. Reduced drug metabolism → exaggerated sedative/CNS depressant effects with medications. Well-controlled: treat normally. Uncontrolled/untreated: avoid elective procedures — cardiovascular compromise risk.

Hyperthyroidism: Accelerated tooth eruption, premature exfoliation of primary teeth, osteoporosis (increased caries risk due to enamel hypoplasia), increased anxiety and restlessness (make short appointments, consider sedation). Cardiovascular effects: tachycardia, hypertension, arrhythmia — avoid adrenaline in uncontrolled hyperthyroidism (risk of thyroid storm).

Thyroid storm (crisis): Rare medical emergency. Fever, tachycardia, hypertension, agitation, tremor, altered consciousness. Can be precipitated by surgery, infection, stress. Emergency referral → hospital. Do not proceed with dental treatment.

Adrenal insufficiency & steroid cover ▾

Primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease): Autoimmune destruction of adrenal cortex. Reduced cortisol and aldosterone. Elevated ACTH (melanocyte-stimulating hormone cross-reaction) → skin and mucosal hyperpigmentation.

Oral sign of Addison's: Patchy brown/black pigmentation of buccal mucosa, gingiva, lips, hard palate. May be the FIRST clinical sign before skin changes. Occurs in non-pigmented individuals (key — distinguish from racial pigmentation). Distribution is irregular and blotchy.

Secondary adrenal insufficiency: Most common cause in clinical practice = iatrogenic (exogenous corticosteroid therapy → HPA axis suppression → reduced endogenous cortisol production). Cannot respond to physiological stress without supplementation → Addisonian crisis risk.

Steroid cover protocol (SDCEP guidelines):

  • ≥5mg prednisolone/day (or equivalent) for ≥1 month: At risk of adrenal suppression
  • Minor dental procedure (routine extractions, periodontal surgery under LA): Double usual morning oral steroid dose on day of procedure only
  • Major dental surgery or GA: IV hydrocortisone 25–50mg at induction, then continue doubled oral dose for 24–48h post-op

Addisonian crisis in chair: Collapse, severe hypotension, tachycardia, pallor, confusion. Management: Lay flat, elevate legs. Call 999. IM hydrocortisone 200mg (Solu-Cortef) immediately if available. IV saline. Monitor. Emergency hospital admission.

Haematological Conditions

Anaemias — oral manifestations ▾

Common oral features across all anaemias: Pallor of oral mucosa (buccal mucosa, lips, conjunctiva), glossitis (smooth, red, burning tongue — depapillated — atrophic glossitis), angular cheilitis, recurrent aphthous ulcers, oral candidiasis (immune compromise), impaired healing.

Iron deficiency anaemia (IDA): Microcytic hypochromic anaemia. Most common anaemia worldwide. Paterson-Kelly (Plummer-Vinson) syndrome: IDA + dysphagia (postcricoid oesophageal web) + glossitis + angular cheilitis + koilonychia (spoon nails). PRE-MALIGNANT for oesophageal/postcricoid SCC.

B12/Folate deficiency: Megaloblastic (macrocytic) anaemia. Glossitis particularly prominent — smooth, red, painful tongue. Can cause peripheral neuropathy and subacute combined degeneration of spinal cord. Pernicious anaemia = B12 deficiency from autoimmune destruction of gastric parietal cells → absent intrinsic factor → reduced B12 absorption. Anti-parietal cell antibodies, anti-intrinsic factor antibodies. Associated with other autoimmune conditions.

Sickle cell disease: Autosomal recessive HbS mutation → vaso-occlusive crises → bone infarcts (can affect jaw → severe pain mimicking dental pain), osteomyelitis risk, hyposplenism → increased infection risk, avascular necrosis. Radiographic: "stepladder" trabecular pattern (perpendicular trabeculae — hair-on-end appearance on skull lateral). Pallor, jaundice, scleral icterus intraorally. Dental management: avoid hypoxia, ensure excellent LA, avoid rubber dam in children, pre-op haematology review, penicillin V prophylaxis (hyposplenism).

Leukaemia — oral manifestations ▾

Oral manifestations (may be presenting features):

  • Gingival infiltration: Especially AML-M5 (monocytic subtype). Generalised gingival swelling — pale, firm, diffuse enlargement of entire gingiva. Leukaemic cells infiltrate gingival tissues directly.
  • Spontaneous gingival bleeding: Thrombocytopenia → petechiae, ecchymoses, prolonged bleeding
  • Oral ulceration: Neutropenia → reduced infection control → opportunistic ulcers
  • Opportunistic infections: Herpes simplex (severe), candida (extensive), CMV
  • Lymphadenopathy: Generalised, non-tender
  • Pallor: Anaemia of chronic disease

Dental management during chemotherapy:

  • Pre-treatment: Full dental assessment. Extract ALL hopeless or failing teeth. Complete restorative work. Optimal OH. Remove all dentures if mucositis expected.
  • During chemotherapy: Check blood count before procedures: Neutrophils >1.0×10⁹ and Platelets >50×10⁹ for surgical procedures (higher threshold preferable). Prophylactic antifungals and antivirals. CHX mouthwash. Excellent OH instruction. Treat mucositis supportively.
  • MRONJ awareness: Some chemotherapy agents and bisphosphonates may be given alongside → increased MRONJ risk if dento-alveolar surgery needed.
Bleeding disorders — haemophilia & VWD ▾

Haemophilia A: X-linked recessive. Factor VIII deficiency. Incidence 1:5000 males. Prolonged APTT, normal PT and bleeding time. Severity: Mild (>5% activity), Moderate (1–5%), Severe (<1%).

Haemophilia B (Christmas disease): X-linked recessive. Factor IX deficiency. Clinically identical to A. Less common (1:30,000).

Clinical features: Deep tissue bleeds (haemarthroses — knee, elbow, ankle), muscle haematomas, intracranial bleed (rare but serious), spontaneous bleeding in severe disease. Soft tissue/mucosal bleeding after dental procedures.

Dental management: MUST be managed in coordination with haemophilia centre (specialist haematologist). Investigations: factor level, inhibitor screen. Options:

  • DDAVP (desmopressin): For mild Haemophilia A only. Releases endogenous FVIII and vWF from storage. IV 0.3μg/kg or intranasal. Tachyphylaxis (loses effect with repeated doses within 48h).
  • Factor concentrate: Recombinant FVIII (A) or FIX (B). Aim for factor level >50% for dental extractions. Administer immediately before procedure.
  • Tranexamic acid: Antifibrinolytic — inhibits plasminogen activation → stabilises clot. 10mg/kg IV pre-procedure OR 4.8% mouthwash QDS × 5–7 days post-procedure.
  • Local haemostasis: Meticulous surgical technique, oxidised cellulose, sutures, avoid aspirin/NSAIDs absolutely.

Von Willebrand disease (VWD): Most common inherited bleeding disorder (1:100 prevalence). Autosomal dominant (mostly). Deficient or defective vWF → impaired primary haemostasis (platelet adhesion) + reduced FVIII (VWF carries/protects FVIII). Prolonged bleeding time, mildly prolonged APTT. Types: 1 (quantitative ↓, 70%), 2 (qualitative defect), 3 (severe, absent vWF). Management: DDAVP (effective for Type 1 and some Type 2), VWF concentrate, tranexamic acid, avoid NSAIDs.

Immune & Gastrointestinal Conditions

HIV/AIDS — oral manifestations ▾

Strongly associated with HIV/AIDS (CDC indicator conditions):

  • Oral hairy leukoplakia (OHL): EBV-associated white corrugated/hairy lesion on LATERAL borders of tongue, bilateral. Cannot be wiped off. Almost exclusively in HIV+ patients. Indicates significant immunosuppression. Responds to antiviral therapy.
  • Pseudomembranous candidiasis: Most common HIV-associated oral lesion. Indicates CD4 <200/μL (AIDS).
  • Kaposi's sarcoma (KS): HHV-8-associated vascular tumour. Red/purple flat or raised lesion — palate (most common intraoral site), gingiva. May be first presentation of AIDS in MSM.
  • Linear gingival erythema (LGE): Distinct fiery red band along free gingival margin, disproportionate to plaque levels. Candida-associated.
  • Necrotising ulcerative periodontitis (NUP): Severe, rapid attachment and bone loss. HIV with severe immunosuppression.

Also seen: Non-Hodgkin's lymphoma (intraoral), HPV-associated condylomata/papillomas, extensive HSV, CMV ulcers, major aphthous ulcers (large, prolonged), xerostomia (from ARV drugs — particularly older NRTIs), bilateral parotid enlargement (lymphoepithelial cysts).

Dental management: Universal precautions (no additional requirements beyond standard infection control). Check medications (ARV interactions). CD4 <200/μL — consider antibiotic cover for surgical procedures, antifungal/antiviral prophylaxis. Viral load and CD4 count guide treatment decisions. Refer to HIV clinician for complex cases.

Sjögren's syndrome — full clinical picture ▾

Primary Sjögren's (sicca syndrome): Sicca symptoms alone (dry eyes + dry mouth). F:M = 9:1. Peak onset: 4th–5th decade. B-cell lymphoma risk (44× higher than general population — primarily MALT type in parotid).

Secondary Sjögren's: Sicca symptoms + another autoimmune condition (most commonly RA, SLE, primary biliary cholangitis, systemic sclerosis).

Oral manifestations: Xerostomia (most prominent symptom — difficulty speaking, swallowing, wearing dentures), accelerated cervical dental caries (cervical and incisal — unusual pattern due to absent salivary buffering and protective functions), recurrent oral candidiasis, atrophic glossitis, bilateral parotid/submandibular enlargement (due to lymphocytic infiltration), dysphagia, dysphonia, difficulty wearing dentures.

Dental management:

  • Salivary substitutes (Biotène gels/spray, carboxymethylcellulose-based artificial saliva)
  • Sialogogues: Pilocarpine 5mg TDS (muscarinic M1/M3 agonist — stimulates residual salivary function; CI: asthma, narrow-angle glaucoma, bradycardia)
  • High-concentration fluoride toothpaste (5000ppm or 2800ppm) + Tooth Mousse (CPP-ACP)
  • Frequent recall (6-monthly minimum) and early aggressive caries management
  • Topical antifungals if candida
  • Advise: sip water frequently, chew sugar-free gum to stimulate saliva, avoid alcohol/tobacco, avoid dry air
  • Monitor for parotid lymphoma (rapidly enlarging, hard, unilateral parotid mass = urgent referral)
Crohn's disease & orofacial granulomatosis (OFG) ▾

Orofacial granulomatosis (OFG): Non-caseating granulomatous inflammation of oro-facial tissues without identifiable systemic cause. May be a localised form of Crohn's, a reaction to dietary antigens (benzoates, cinnamon, cochineal), or idiopathic.

Oral Crohn's / OFG manifestations (may precede GI diagnosis by years):

  • Lip swelling: Persistent, diffuse, rubbery, non-pitting oedema — especially lower lip (orofacial oedema). Classical "cobblestone" appearance of mucosa.
  • Tag-like mucosal hypertrophy: Hyperplastic tissue folds in buccal sulcus
  • Mucosal tags: Polypoid projections near posterior buccal mucosa or sulcus
  • Linear fissuring ulcers in sulci: Deep, linear, vertical ulcers in upper or lower buccal sulcus — most SPECIFIC oral sign of Crohn's
  • Cobblestoning of buccal mucosa: Oedematous folds giving a pebbly surface
  • Gingival swelling: Oedematous, hyperaemic, granulomatous gingival changes

Histology: Non-caseating epithelioid granulomata in submucosa — same as sarcoidosis and TB histologically (need clinical/serological differentiation).

Management: Dietary modification (eliminate common triggers: cinnamon, chocolate, cola, benzoates). Intralesional triamcinolone injection into lip swelling. Topical steroids. Systemic: prednisolone, methotrexate, infliximab (anti-TNF). GI investigation and multidisciplinary management. Refer to gastroenterology.

Coeliac disease — oral manifestations ▾

Gluten-sensitive enteropathy. HLA-DQ2/DQ8 association. Autoimmune villous atrophy of small intestine → malabsorption.

Oral manifestations:

  • Dental enamel hypoplasia: Symmetric, bilateral, affects permanent teeth in childhood. Horizontal bands of pitting/grooving or diffuse opacity — secondary to calcium/vitamin D malabsorption during critical enamel development period. Characteristic distribution across all four quadrants (compared to localised Turner's hypoplasia). Pathognomonic for systemic cause.
  • Recurrent aphthous ulcers: Most common trigger for investigation. May be the only presenting feature. Resolves with gluten-free diet.
  • Atrophic glossitis: From B12, iron, folate deficiency
  • Angular cheilitis
  • Delayed dental development: Malabsorption of nutrients required for tooth formation

Diagnosis: Anti-tissue transglutaminase (anti-tTG IgA) + total IgA (if IgA deficient → use IgG-based tests). Confirm with duodenal biopsy (Marsh criteria). HLA typing.

Management: Lifelong strict gluten-free diet → oral lesions improve. Replace nutritional deficiencies. Monitor for associated conditions (T-cell lymphoma, osteoporosis, other autoimmune).

Neurological & Other Conditions

Epilepsy — management & drug effects ▾

Dental management: Confirm seizure type and control status. Never treat uncontrolled epilepsy electively. Dental trauma (avulsions, crown fractures) is common — assess patients for previous trauma signs. Morning appointments. Use rubber dam (but ensure suction and airway access). Aspirator and instruments secured. Avoid prolonged procedures. Identify stressors/flicker risk (avoid bright lights or flickering overhead light).

Drug effects on oral cavity:

  • Phenytoin: Gingival hyperplasia in 50% of patients. Severity related to plaque control (exacerbated by poor OH) and drug plasma level. Starts at interdental papillae, extends across gingiva, may cover teeth. Management: excellent OH, possible drug substitution (discuss with neurologist — switch to carbamazepine/lamotrigine), gingivectomy if severe and well-controlled epilepsy.
  • Carbamazepine: Agranulocytosis (rare but dangerous — watch for unexplained oral infections/ulcers), hyponatraemia, aplastic anaemia (monitor FBC), Stevens-Johnson syndrome/TEN.
  • Sodium valproate: Thrombocytopenia (check platelets before surgery), hepatotoxicity, teratogenic (neural tube defects — check pregnancy status).
  • Lamotrigine: Stevens-Johnson syndrome (rare), dizziness. Fewer oral side effects.

Seizure management in dental chair: (1) Call for help. (2) Remove instruments from mouth. (3) Lower chair to flat position. (4) Cushion head — do NOT restrain. (5) Do NOT put anything in mouth. (6) Protect from injury. (7) Time the seizure. (8) If >5 minutes: call 999, administer buccal midazolam 10mg (preferred) or rectal diazepam 10mg (adults). (9) Reassure when consciousness returns. (10) Arrange transport home — do not drive.

Trigeminal neuralgia — diagnosis & treatment ▾

Classic TN (CVN — cranial nerve compression): Unilateral paroxysmal severe electric shock/stabbing pain <2 minutes in trigeminal distribution. Most commonly V2 (maxillary) and/or V3 (mandibular). Trigger zones: light touch from eating, speaking, cold wind, brushing teeth, smiling. Pain-free intervals (characteristic). No neurological deficit.

Cause: Most commonly vascular compression of trigeminal nerve root entry zone at pons — usually by superior cerebellar artery. Neurovascular contact → demyelination → aberrant electrical transmission.

Must exclude: Dental causes first (refer back if any dental pathology). MS (younger patients — bilateral TN or associated neurological signs). Space-occupying lesion/acoustic neuroma (neurological signs, gradual onset, persistent background pain).

Treatment:

  • Carbamazepine (first-line): 100mg BD → titrate to 400–800mg BD. Side effects: dizziness, ataxia, diplopia (dose-related), agranulocytosis (monitor FBC), hyponatraemia, teratogenic. 70% efficacy initially.
  • Oxcarbazepine: Better tolerated than carbamazepine, fewer interactions. 300mg BD → 600mg BD.
  • Second-line: Gabapentin, pregabalin, lamotrigine, baclofen (muscle relaxant, useful adjunct).
  • Surgical: Microvascular decompression (MVD) — most effective, longest lasting, requires craniotomy. Gamma Knife radiosurgery (non-invasive), Percutaneous rhizotomy (glycerol injection, balloon compression, radiofrequency thermocoagulation).
Burning mouth syndrome (BMS) ▾

Definition: Chronic intraoral burning or dysaesthesia WITHOUT visible mucosal lesion or identifiable local disease causing it. Diagnosis of exclusion.

Demographics: Post-menopausal women most commonly (50–70 years). Female:Male = 3:1 to 7:1. Significant psychological component (anxiety, depression, hypochondriasis common).

Exclude treatable causes first (secondary BMS): Iron/B12/folate/zinc deficiency, erythematous candida, xerostomia (from drugs or Sjögren's), denture allergy (PMMA base or denture cleansers), lichen planus, hypersensitivity reactions, diabetes mellitus, hypothyroidism, anxiety/depression.

Types: Type 1 (diurnal — no burning on waking, builds through day, peak evening — most common, suggests peripheral neuropathy). Type 2 (constant throughout day — associated psychological factors). Type 3 (intermittent — possibly allergic).

Treatment: Thorough explanation and reassurance (most important — many patients fear cancer). Low-dose clonazepam 0.5–1mg (rinse and spit for 3 minutes then spit — avoids systemic effects; or systemic). Alpha-lipoic acid supplements. Cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT). Low-dose amitriptyline 10–25mg at night. Capsaicin (controversial). Systemic CBT/psychotherapy for significant psychological overlay.

Parkinson's disease & dementia — oral considerations ▾

Parkinson's disease oral features: Sialorrhoea/drooling (due to impaired swallowing, not excess production), dysphagia (aspiration risk), poor OH maintenance (tremor, rigidity, reduced dexterity), xerostomia (from anticholinergic medications), dysgeusia, bruxism, occlusal problems (dystonic movements).

Dental management: Short appointments adapted to "on" drug phase (when medication is working well — ask patient optimal time). Physical support for head and jaw during treatment. Adapted oral hygiene aids (electric toothbrush, grip modifications). Use rubber dam with care. Avoid antipsychotics (dopamine D2 antagonists — worsen Parkinson's symptoms). Opioids → increased sedation risk. Avoid prolonged supine position (aspiration risk).

Dementia: Progressive loss of cooperation and communication. Assess cognitive function and capacity. Involve carer. Minimise invasive treatment — prioritise pain management and infection control. Preventive focus early (when cooperation better). Power of attorney considerations. Document treatment discussions carefully. Simple, clear communication.
