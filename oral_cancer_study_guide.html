Definition & Anatomy

🔍 What is Oral Cancer?

Malignancies arising from the oral cavity mucosa. Distinct from oropharyngeal cancer (soft palate, posterior 1/3 tongue/base of tongue, tonsils, posterior pharyngeal wall). Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) accounts for ~90% of oral cancers. The two must be distinguished as they have different risk factors, staging systems, prognosis, and management.

🦷 Oral Cavity Boundaries

Anteriorly: Vermilion border of lips → mucosal surface of lips (inner surface) | Posteriorly: Junction of hard/soft palate (superior) + circumvallate papillae of tongue (inferior) | Laterally: Buccal mucosa | Superiorly: Hard palate and upper alveolus | Inferiorly: Floor of mouth

Oral Cavity Sub-sites (7 Key Areas)

👄 Lips — vermilion border inward (upper + lower)

👅 Anterior 2/3 tongue (oral tongue) — anterior to circumvallate papillae

🦷 Floor of mouth — U-shaped area inferior to tongue

😮 Buccal mucosa — inner lining of cheeks and lips

🏛️ Hard palate — anterior bony roof of mouth

🦷 Gingiva / Alveolar ridges — upper and lower

📐 Retromolar trigone — small triangular mucosa posterior to last molar, overlying anterior ramus of mandible

Epidemiology

🌍 Global Statistics
  • ~377,000 new cases annually worldwide
  • ~177,000 deaths annually
  • Highest incidence: South & Southeast Asia (India, Pakistan, Bangladesh — betel nut use)
  • Also high: Eastern Europe, South America
  • Rising incidence in younger adults globally (HPV)
🇺🇸 United States
  • ~54,000 new oral/pharyngeal cases/year
  • ~10,800 deaths/year
  • Male:Female ~2:1 (gap narrowing due to HPV)
  • Peak incidence: 6th–7th decade
  • Rare before age 40 (unless HPV-related)
  • African Americans: worse stage at presentation, worse survival

Pathogenesis

⚗️ Stepwise Progression

Normal epithelium → Epithelial Dysplasia (mild→moderate→severe) → Carcinoma in Situ (CIS) → Invasive SCC → Metastasis


Two main carcinogenic pathways:

  • Tobacco/Alcohol pathway: PAH & nitrosamines form DNA adducts → TP53 mutations → loss of cell cycle checkpoint control → uncontrolled proliferation
  • HPV pathway: E6 oncoprotein degrades p53 protein; E7 oncoprotein inactivates Rb (retinoblastoma protein) → cell cycle dysregulation → malignant transformation
🧬 Key Molecular Alterations
  • TP53 mutations (most common — 50–80% of cases)
  • EGFR overexpression (>90% of HNSCCs)
  • CDKN2A (p16) inactivation
  • Cyclin D1 amplification (11q13)
  • Loss of heterozygosity: 3p, 9p, 17p (early events)
  • VEGF overexpression (angiogenesis)
🔴 Field Cancerization (Slaughter, 1953)

Entire upper aerodigestive tract exposed to same carcinogens → "condemned mucosa" → multiple independent potentially malignant areas → synchronous or metachronous primary tumors


Risk of 2nd primary: 3–7% per year — lifelong surveillance essential!

Common 2nd primaries: Larynx, esophagus, lung (especially in smokers)

Routes of Spread

➡️ Local Extension

Direct invasion of adjacent mucosa, muscles, bone, cartilage, and nerves (perineural spread)

🔗 Lymphatic Spread

Most common route. Regional cervical LN (Levels I–V). Skip metastasis possible (tongue → Level IV, bypassing III)

🩸 Haematogenous (Late/Rare)

Lung (most common) → Liver → Bone → Skin/soft tissue. Usually indicates Stage IVC.

Histologic Grades:

  • G1 — Well differentiated: Abundant keratin pearls, resembles normal squamous epithelium, lower mitotic rate. Relatively better prognosis.
  • G2 — Moderately differentiated: Some keratin production, nuclear pleomorphism, moderate mitoses.
  • G3 — Poorly differentiated: Little or no keratin, marked pleomorphism, high mitotic rate, high nuclear:cytoplasmic ratio. Aggressive.
  • G4 — Undifferentiated: Cannot identify squamous features. Worst prognosis.

Macroscopic patterns:

  • Exophytic (fungating): Outward growing cauliflower-like mass — lower grade, better prognosis
  • Ulcerative: Crater with raised, rolled, everted indurated margins — classic presentation
  • Infiltrative/Endophytic: Submucosal spread, deeper invasion, worse prognosis

⚠️ Classic Clinical Description

Oral SCC presents as an indurated, non-healing ulcer with raised, rolled, everted, hard margins. The induration (woody firmness on palpation) reflects desmoplastic stromal reaction and tumor infiltration — a cardinal sign of malignancy.

SCC Subtypes of note:

  • Verrucous SCC — see separate card below (low-grade variant)
  • Basaloid SCC — aggressive, oropharynx more common, HPV+
  • Papillary SCC — exophytic, better prognosis
  • Spindle cell carcinoma (sarcomatoid) — biphasic, aggressive, post-RT setting
  • Adenosquamous carcinoma — rare, aggressive mixed type
  • Acantholytic SCC — pseudoglandular appearance, aggressive

2. Mucoepidermoid Carcinoma (MEC)

MOST COMMON salivary gland malignancy overall
  • Minor SG site: Palate most common (parotid overall)
  • Grades: Low (cystic, mucous cells dominant → good prognosis) | Intermediate | High (solid, epidermoid dominant → poor prognosis)
  • Histology: Three cell types — mucous, epidermoid (squamoid), intermediate cells
  • Molecular: t(11;19)(q21;p13) → MAML2-CRTC1 fusion gene (most cases)
  • Low-grade: Often cystic, may be mistaken for mucocele

3. Polymorphous Low-Grade Adenocarcinoma (PLGA)

  • Exclusively minor salivary glands — never occurs in major glands
  • Posterior hard palate most common oral site
  • Histologically diverse growth patterns (hence "polymorphous") — tubular, papillary, cribriform, solid
  • Despite diverse pattern, cytology is bland and monotonous (unlike ACC)
  • Low-grade, rarely metastasizes, good prognosis with surgery
  • May show perineural invasion but less aggressive than ACC

Other / Less Common Oral Malignancies

🖤 Primary Oral Melanoma <1%
  • Primary mucosal melanoma — NO UV etiology (unlike cutaneous)
  • Sites: Hard palate + maxillary gingiva (most common oral sites)
  • Presents as pigmented patch → nodule → ulceration + satellite lesions
  • Prognosis: Very poor — 5-year survival 15–20%
  • Often deeply invasive at diagnosis (depth of invasion = key prognostic factor)
  • No Clark's levels (mucosal melanoma staging system different)
  • Treatment: Wide excision ± immunotherapy (ipilimumab + nivolumab) for metastatic
  • BRAF mutation testing (present in some cases)
🔵 NHL Lymphoma NHL
  • Diffuse Large B-Cell Lymphoma (DLBCL) most common type in H&N
  • Waldeyer's ring (tonsils) = most common H&N extranodal site overall
  • Oral cavity proper: gingiva, palate, tongue, floor of mouth
  • Non-healing soft tissue mass without SCC features; may be purple-red
  • Diagnosis: Excisional biopsy + immunohistochemistry
  • Treatment: R-CHOP chemotherapy ± RT
  • Prognosis depends on histologic type and stage
💜 Kaposi Sarcoma HHV-8 / HIV
  • Vascular neoplasm driven by HHV-8 (Human Herpesvirus 8)
  • Hard palate most common oral site — classic location
  • Purple-red, violaceous, painless flat/nodular lesion
  • Non-tender; may ulcerate with progression
  • Part of AIDS-defining illnesses
  • Treatment: ART (antiretroviral therapy) — often lesions regress; local RT, intralesional chemo, systemic chemo for advanced cases
🦴 Metastatic Tumors to Jaws
  • Jaw mets more common than metastasis to oral soft tissue
  • Most common primaries: Breast, Lung, Kidney, Colon, Thyroid
  • Mandible >> Maxilla (posterior body/angle most common)
  • May present as: jaw pain, paresthesia, loose teeth, pathological fracture
  • Numb chin syndrome = numbness of chin/lower lip due to mandibular mets compressing inferior alveolar nerve — BAD prognostic sign
  • Treatment: Palliative (primary tumour management)

Detailed Site-by-Site Analysis

👅 Tongue (Anterior 2/3)
MOST COMMON SITE (Developed World)

Subsites at risk: Lateral border (most common) > ventral surface > dorsum (rare)

Why early LN spread? Tongue has the richest lymphatic network of any oral cavity site — tumors as small as T1 can have occult nodal metastases in 20–30% of cases

Bilateral spread: Midline or tip lesions can drain bilaterally → treat BOTH sides of neck

Tongue immobility: Fixed tongue = extrinsic muscle invasion = at least T4a

Referred otalgia: Lingual nerve → Jacobson's nerve (CN IX) → tympanic plexus → ear pain

Lymph node drainage levels:

  • Tip → Submental (Level IA)
  • Lateral border → Submandibular (IB) → Upper jugular (IIA)
  • Skip metastasis to Level IV occurs (bypass III)
  • Bilateral drainage in midline/tip lesions
⚠️ Key Clinical Point

Always perform elective neck dissection for tongue cancers T2 and above (or T1 with DOI >4mm) — occult node mets are common even in clinically N0 necks.

🕳️ Floor of Mouth
2nd Most Common Site

U-shaped region inferior to the tongue, bounded by lower alveolar ridge. Contains sublingual glands, submandibular (Wharton's) ducts, lingual nerves, and CN XII. Proximity to mandibular symphysis means early bone invasion is common. FOM tumors often cross the midline and can involve the contralateral side → bilateral neck management often required.

Clinical signs: Mass under tongue, tongue displacement upward, Wharton's duct obstruction (sialadenitis), inability to protrude tongue (CN XII invasion), bimanual palpation reveals submucosal induration before visible lesion.

💋 Lip
BEST PROGNOSIS (~90% 5-year)

Lower lip (90% of lip cancers): SCC. UV radiation is the primary carcinogen — fair-skinned patients, outdoor occupations, pipe/cigar smoking. Actinic cheilitis is the precursor lesion. Lower lip is sun-exposed (angled toward sun).

Upper lip (10%): Less sun-exposed. SCC still occurs. BCC can develop at the cutaneous-vermilion junction. Tends to present at earlier stage with better prognosis.

Why the excellent prognosis?

  • Highly visible location → patients notice early
  • Slow-growing lesions
  • Lower rate of cervical LN mets vs other sites
  • Late presentation is less common
  • Easier to examine by clinician
😮 Buccal Mucosa
Most Common Site in South / SE Asia

Inner lining of cheeks and lips (excluding skin). SCC and verrucous carcinoma are both common here, strongly associated with tobacco chewing and betel nut use. Tumors here can be deceptively large due to the expansile nature of the buccal space. Adjacent structures at risk include the pterygomandibular raphe, buccinator muscle, and pterygoid muscles (→ trismus). Drains to buccal and submandibular nodes.

🏛️ Hard Palate
Minor Salivary Gland Tumour Hotspot

Hard palate has the highest density of minor salivary glands in the oral cavity (~400–800 per cm²), explaining the predominance of salivary gland tumors here. When SCC occurs, it tends to present late due to the bony hard palate resisting invasion initially (then erodes into nasal floor or maxillary sinus). Melanoma has a predilection for hard palate and maxillary gingiva. Lymphatic drainage goes to retropharyngeal, upper jugular, and submandibular nodes.

🦷 Gingiva / Alveolar Ridges
Frequently Misdiagnosed

Common in edentulous patients. The classic pitfall: clinicians and patients attribute gingival lesions to denture irritation or periodontal disease → delayed diagnosis is a known problem. Bone invasion occurs early due to the intimate relationship between gingival mucosa and underlying alveolar bone. Loose teeth and non-healing extraction sockets are classic presentations. Lower alveolar ridge more commonly affected than upper.

📐 Retromolar Trigone (RMT)
WORST PROGNOSIS — 30–40% 5-year

Despite being a small anatomical area, the RMT is surrounded by critical structures: ramus of mandible (posteriorly), medial and lateral pterygoid muscles (superiorly), buccinator muscle (anteriorly), pterygomandibular raphe. This explains why even small RMT tumors present with trismus (pterygoid involvement) and achieve T4a classification quickly. Bone invasion is early. Surgical resection often requires mandibular resection and extensive reconstruction.

🔴 Classic Sign of RMT Cancer

Trismus (limited mouth opening <35mm interincisally) = pterygoid/masticator space involvement. Once trismus develops, the disease is at least T4a and surgical access is challenging.

💡 Tobacco + Alcohol Synergy — Critical Concept

The combination is not additive but synergistic. Together they increase oral cancer risk by 15–30 times. Mechanism: alcohol acts as a solvent increasing carcinogen penetration; acetaldehyde (alcohol metabolite) is itself carcinogenic; combined effects overwhelm DNA repair mechanisms.

1️⃣ Leukoplakia
1–17% malignant transformation (~5% average)
  • Definition: White patch/plaque on oral mucosa that CANNOT be characterised clinically or pathologically as any other definable lesion (diagnosis of exclusion — rule out all other causes first)
  • Types: Homogeneous (flat, uniformly white, smooth surface — lower risk) | Non-homogeneous (speckled, nodular, verrucous, exophytic — higher risk) | Proliferative Verrucous Leukoplakia (PVL) (multifocal, progressive, highest transformation — 60–70%+)
  • High-risk features for transformation: Floor of mouth or ventral tongue location, size >2cm, non-homogeneous type, histologic dysplasia (especially severe), female sex, non-smoker (paradox — non-smoker leukoplakia transforms more often)
  • Histology: WHO grading — mild dysplasia (changes confined to lower 1/3), moderate (lower 2/3), severe/CIS (full thickness). Or Ljubljana binary: low-risk vs high-risk
  • Management: Remove causative factor → incisional biopsy → if mild dysplasia, monitor every 3–6 months → moderate/severe dysplasia or CIS → surgical excision → lifelong follow-up
2️⃣ Erythroplakia
40–50% malignant transformation — HIGHEST RISK PMD
  • Definition: Bright red, velvety patch on oral mucosa that CANNOT be attributed to any other definable cause
  • Often already severely dysplastic, CIS, or frankly invasive SCC at time of first biopsy (~80% have severe histologic changes)
  • Rare condition but extremely dangerous — do NOT ignore or dismiss a red oral patch
  • Common sites: Floor of mouth, soft palate, ventral tongue
  • Appears bright red/fiery red, velvety texture, well-demarcated but irregular borders
  • Management: URGENT incisional biopsy — treat as cancer until proven otherwise. Excision if malignant/CIS.
3️⃣ Oral Submucous Fibrosis (OSMF)
7–13% malignant transformation
  • Etiology: Areca nut (betel nut) chewing — arecoline → ↑ collagen synthesis + ↓ collagenase activity → fibrosis; Pan masala (with tobacco) dramatically increases risk
  • Classic triad: (1) Burning sensation in mouth, (2) Blanching of oral mucosa, (3) Progressive trismus
  • Progressive features: Buccal mucosa becomes stiff and pale → fibrotic bands palpable → restricted mouth opening → difficulty swallowing
  • IRREVERSIBLE: Once fibrosis is established, it CANNOT be reversed — only symptomatic management possible
  • Staging (Pindborg & Sirsat): Stage 1 (burning, no fibrosis) → Stage 2 (mild fibrosis) → Stage 3 (moderate fibrosis with trismus) → Stage 4 (severe trismus <15mm, extensive fibrosis)
  • Management: Absolute cessation of areca nut use, intralesional corticosteroid injections (triamcinolone), pentoxifylline (vasodilator), lycopene, colchicine, physiotherapy (mouth-opening exercises), surgical release of fibrotic bands + splinting for severe trismus
4️⃣ Oral Lichen Planus (OLP)
0.5–2% malignant transformation
  • 6 clinical types: Reticular (most common — Wickham's striae on bilateral buccal mucosa) | Papular | Plaque-like | Erosive (painful, irregular red ulcers) | Atrophic | Bullous
  • Wickham's striae: Interlacing white lines/network pattern = pathognomonic of reticular OLP
  • PMD status: WHO 2005 included OLP — erosive & atrophic forms have highest transformation risk (0.5–2%). Reticular form has very low risk.
  • Bilateral buccal mucosa most common site; also tongue, gingiva (desquamative gingivitis)
  • Histology: Band-like lymphocytic infiltrate at epithelial-connective tissue junction, "saw tooth" rete ridges, civatte bodies (apoptotic cells)
  • Management: Topical corticosteroids (first line: triamcinolone acetonide), topical tacrolimus, systemic steroids for severe flares. Lifelong monitoring — every 6 months
5️⃣ Actinic Cheilitis
~10% malignant transformation
  • Chronic UV-induced (actinic) damage to the lower lip vermilion (lip = most sun-exposed oral region)
  • Pathognomonic feature: Blurring/loss of definition of the vermilion border (where lip meets skin)
  • Features: Dry, scaly, rough lip with areas of erythema and white patches; may show focal ulceration
  • Risk factors: Fair skin (Fitzpatrick I–II), outdoor occupation (farmers, fishermen), high UV exposure, history of sunburn, male sex
  • Histology: Solar elastosis, epithelial atrophy or hyperkeratosis, basal layer atypia
  • Management: Sunscreen, lip balm, avoid sun exposure, vermilionectomy (lip shave — definitive treatment, histopathological examination of entire lip), topical 5-FU (5-fluorouracil), CO2 laser ablation, photodynamic therapy (PDT)

Important Clinical Signs

🪨 Induration

Woody firmness on palpation caused by tumor infiltration + desmoplastic stromal reaction. The hallmark of oral malignancy. Always palpate — some tumors are more palpable than visible.

👂 Referred Otalgia

Ear pain from oral cancer without ear pathology. Tongue/FOM: lingual nerve → auriculotemporal nerve (V3) or tympanic branch CN IX. Rule: Always examine the oral cavity when a patient complains of unilateral ear pain with a normal ear exam.

🦷 Trismus

Limited mouth opening <35mm. Caused by pterygoid/masticator space invasion. Indicates advanced disease (T4a or T4b). Measure with ruler at incisors interincisally. Normal: 35–45mm. Also caused by RT fibrosis (late complication).

😶 Vincent's Symptom

Numbness or paresthesia of the lower lip and chin. Caused by inferior alveolar nerve or mental nerve involvement. Strongly suggests mandibular bone invasion or perineural spread. Also occurs with metastatic disease to mandible ("numb chin syndrome").

😮 Tongue Fixation

Inability to protrude or lateralise the tongue. Caused by invasion of extrinsic tongue muscles (genioglossus, hyoglossus, etc.) or CN XII. Classifies as T4a or greater.

🔵 Fixation to Bone

Tumor adherent to mandible or maxilla — clinically felt as immobility of mass on manipulation. Suggests cortical bone invasion (marginal mandibulectomy minimum) or medullary invasion (segmental resection needed).

🚩 Red & Green Flags
Clinical indicators for urgent referral vs reassurance
🔴 RED FLAGS — Urgent 2-Week Referral Required

Any of the following features in a patient mandate urgent referral to an oral & maxillofacial surgeon or head and neck oncology team. Do NOT delay with "watch and wait."

🔴
Non-healing ulcer >3 weeks — most important red flag; oral cancer must be actively excluded
🔴
Red patch (Erythroplakia) — highest malignant potential of any PMD (40–50%); treat as cancer until proven otherwise
🔴
Mixed red and white patches (Erythroleukoplakia) — high malignant potential; biopsy urgently
🔴
Unexplained tooth mobility — suggests bone destruction by invading tumor (always radiograph first)
🔴
Unexplained cervical lymphadenopathy — especially hard, non-tender, progressive, fixed; metastatic disease until proven otherwise
🔴
Trismus (limited mouth opening <35mm) — pterygoid/masticator space invasion = advanced disease; not from dental cause
🔴
Unexplained dysphagia or odynophagia — tumor mass effect or neuromuscular involvement
🔴
Unexplained oral bleeding — ulcerating tumor eroding blood vessel
🔴
Paresthesia or numbness of lip, chin, or tongue — perineural invasion or bone invasion compressing inferior alveolar nerve
🔴
Non-healing extraction socket (>4 weeks) — tumor infiltrating the socket; often mistaken for dry socket initially
🔴
Referred otalgia with normal ear examination — tongue/FOM cancer classic presentation via CN V3 or CN IX/X pathways
🔴
Visible or palpable unexplained oral mass — new, progressive, firm or fixed mass in any oral subsite
🔴
White patch that cannot be rubbed off — distinguishes leukoplakia from pseudomembranous candidiasis (which CAN be wiped off)
🔴
Hoarseness >3 weeks — laryngeal involvement or synchronous second primary
🔴
Unexplained weight loss (>10% over 6 months) — systemic malignant process, cachectic state
🟢 GREEN FLAGS — Lower Urgency (Monitor, Review at 2 Weeks)

These features suggest a benign cause, but always reassess if symptoms persist or worsen. If a "benign" lesion has not fully resolved in 2–3 weeks, refer urgently.

🟢

⚠️ Golden Rule: When in Doubt — Refer and Biopsy

NICE guidance: Any person with unexplained oral ulceration, white patch, or oral lesion lasting more than 3 weeks should be referred on a 2-week urgent cancer pathway. The cost of delayed diagnosis in oral cancer is significant — 5-year survival drops from 80% (Stage I) to <20% (Stage IV).

Clinical Examination

🔍 Key Principle

A complete, systematic examination of ALL mucosal surfaces in good light is mandatory. Remove dentures first. Use gauze to grasp and retract the tongue. Examine the patient in a systematic sequence every time — don't "jump" to the obvious lesion without examining everything else.

👤 Extra-oral Examination
  • Face: Asymmetry, swelling, skin changes, fistula
  • Lips: Inspect vermilion borders, commissures
  • TMJ: Measure mouth opening interincisally (normal: 35–45mm); note deviation
  • Neck: Systematic Level I–VI + posterior triangle + supraclavicular examination
  • LN assessment: Size, consistency (soft/firm/hard/rubbery), mobility (fixed/mobile), tenderness, skin involvement, ENE
  • Cranial nerves: CN V (sensation), CN VII (facial movement), CN IX, X, XI, XII
👄 Intra-oral Examination
  • Lips — inner mucosal surface
  • Buccal mucosa — both sides bilaterally
  • Upper and lower gingivae and alveolar ridges
  • Hard palate — tilt head back, use mirror
  • Tongue — grasp with gauze and flip to inspect lateral borders and ventral surface
  • Floor of mouth — bimanual palpation mandatory (one finger intraoral, hand extraoral)
  • Retromolar area — use mirror/retractor
  • Oropharynx — soft palate, tonsils, posterior tongue base
  • Document: size (mm/cm), site, color, surface texture, border characteristics, consistency, fixation

Biopsy — Gold Standard for Diagnosis

⚠️ Most Important Diagnostic Step

Histopathological examination of a tissue biopsy is the GOLD STANDARD. Clinical diagnosis alone is insufficient. All suspicious oral lesions MUST be biopsied before treatment decisions are made.

✂️ Types of Biopsy
  • Incisional biopsy: Lesions >1cm — representative sample from most suspicious area (erythematous, indurated, ulcerated margin)
  • Excisional biopsy: Lesions <1cm — entire lesion removed with 2–3mm margin
  • Scalpel preferred over punch or laser (laser thermally damages architecture)
  • Fix in 10% neutral buffered formalin
  • Send with completed request form including clinical details
📍 Biopsy Site Selection
  • Sample from RED or erythematous areas within white patch (erythroplakic component)
  • Sample from indurated border of ulcer (NOT the ulcer base)
  • AVOID: Necrotic center (low diagnostic yield, risk of false negative)
  • AVOID: Areas immediately adjacent to major vessels or nerves
  • Toluidine blue staining can identify optimal biopsy site
  • Include normal-to-abnormal transition zone if possible
⛔ NEVER Perform FNA on the Primary Oral Lesion

FNA (Fine Needle Aspiration) is ONLY for cervical lymph nodes. Performing FNA on a primary oral tumour risks tumour seeding along the needle track. Tissue biopsy (incisional/excisional) is required for primary lesion diagnosis.

  • Best for: Bone invasion, cortical erosion, LN assessment, soft tissue extension

  • First-choice for nodal staging and bone involvement
  • Also: Chest CT for pulmonary mets and synchronous lung primaries

  • Best for: Soft tissue detail, perineural spread, tongue/FOM/masticator space
  • T1+fat saturation+gadolinium = gold standard for perineural invasion
  • Superior to CT for posterior tongue, medullary bone invasion, skull base
  • Complementary to CT — often both are used

  • F-18 FDG PET-CT for metabolic activity

  • Not routine for T1–T2, cN0 disease

  • Quick, low-dose screening for mandibular/maxillary bone involvement
  • Shows cortical breach, tooth root resorption

  • LN assessment — morphology, vascularity (Doppler), size

  • Direct laryngoscopy + esophagoscopy + bronchoscopy
  • Detects synchronous second primaries in upper aerodigestive tract (3–10% incidence)
  • Especially important in heavy smokers/drinkers
  • Can be done under same GA as primary surgical resection

📊 TNM Staging — AJCC 8th Edition (2018)
Oral Cavity SCC — T, N, M classification and overall stage groupings
🆕 Key Changes from AJCC 7th to 8th Edition
  • 1. Depth of Invasion (DOI) now incorporated into T staging (replaces simply using tumour size as sole T descriptor)
  • 2. Extranodal Extension (ENE) now incorporated into N staging as N3b — any node with clinical ENE = N3b regardless of size
  • DOI ≠ Tumour thickness: DOI is measured from the basement membrane of the adjacent normal mucosa down to the deepest point of invasion — exophytic tumours have less DOI than their thickness; ulcerative tumours may have greater DOI

💡 Understanding DOI — Clinical Impact

Why DOI matters: DOI is a better independent predictor of cervical LN metastasis and survival than tumour thickness. Key thresholds:

  • DOI >4mm → consider elective neck dissection even in cN0 neck (occult mets risk ~20%+)
  • DOI >5mm → T2 (even if tumour ≤2cm)
  • DOI >10mm → T3 (even if tumour ≤4cm)

Remember: DOI is measured by the pathologist — it cannot be clinically estimated reliably. The DOI in the biopsy report directs staging and treatment planning.

🔴 Extranodal Extension (ENE) — Must-Know Concept

ENE = Tumour cells extending beyond the capsule of the lymph node into surrounding soft tissues.

Clinical signs of ENE: Skin tethering or fixation, cranial nerve involvement, dense fixed neck mass, invasion of adjacent structures (IJV, carotid, strap muscles)

Pathological ENE (pENE): Found on histology of neck dissection specimen — classified as major ENE (>2mm extension) or minor ENE (≤2mm)

Impact: N3b classification, dramatically worsens prognosis, mandates adjuvant concurrent chemoradiation (not RT alone) — per RTOG 9501 and EORTC 22931 trials

⚠️ Critical Mimic: Necrotising Sialometaplasia

Presents as a deep, painful crater-like ulcer on the hard palate, often bilateral. Closely mimics SCC and minor salivary gland malignancy both clinically and histologically (pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia). Key: it is benign and self-limiting, healing spontaneously in 6–10 weeks. Etiology: ischaemic necrosis of minor salivary glands (trauma, smoking, cocaine, dental block injection). Always biopsy to exclude malignancy — do not assume it is NSM without histology.

🔭 Screening
Methods, indications, and evidence for oral cancer screening programmes
✅ Wilson-Jungner Criteria — Oral Cancer Meets Most
  • Important health problem with significant morbidity/mortality ✓
  • Recognisable early/latent/preclinical stage ✓
  • Effective treatment available with better outcomes if caught early ✓
  • Acceptable, safe screening test available ✓
  • Natural history well understood ✓
  • Cost-effective in high-risk populations ✓
  • Adequate facilities for diagnosis and treatment ✓

No national population-level oral cancer screening programme currently exists in the UK or US — primarily due to insufficient RCT evidence for mortality reduction. Opportunistic screening remains the standard.

Who to Screen (High-Risk Populations)

🎯 High-Risk Individuals
  • Age ≥40 years
  • Current or former tobacco users (any form)
  • Heavy alcohol use (>14 units/week)
  • Combined tobacco + alcohol users
  • Betel nut / areca nut users
  • Known HPV infection
  • Previous head and neck cancer (field cancerization)
  • Immunosuppressed patients (HIV, organ transplant)
  • Known oral potentially malignant disorders
  • Patients with Li-Fraumeni syndrome or Fanconi anemia
📅 Recommended Intervals
  • High-risk individuals: Annual comprehensive oral examination
  • Known PMD (leukoplakia, OSMF): Every 3–6 months
  • OLP: Every 6 months
  • Post-treatment surveillance: Every 1–3 months (Year 1) → 2–4 months (Year 2) → 4–6 months (Years 3–5) → 6–12 months (Year 5+)
  • General population: Opportunistic screening at routine dental visits

Screening Methods

1

Conventional Oral Examination (COE) — Gold Standard of Screening

Visual inspection with adequate lighting + bimanual palpation by a trained dental or medical professional. Sensitivity: ~84%, Specificity: ~97%. Most cost-effective method. Recommended annually for high-risk groups. Requires systematic approach, good lighting, and removal of dentures. Adjunct mirror/retractor for retromolar area. This is the cornerstone — all other methods are adjuncts to COE.

2

Toluidine Blue (Tolonium Chloride) Vital Staining

1% aqueous solution applied topically to the entire oral mucosa. Malignant/dysplastic cells stain dark blue (affinity for increased nuclear DNA/RNA in rapidly proliferating cells). Followed by rinse with 1% acetic acid to remove non-specific staining. Positive: retained blue stain after acetic acid rinse. Sensitivity ~80%, Specificity ~70%. False positives: inflammation, trauma, active ulcers. Clinical use: Biopsy site selection within large/multifocal lesions, post-treatment surveillance. Not a replacement for biopsy.

3

VELscope (Visually Enhanced Lesion Scope) — Autofluorescence

Blue-green excitation light (400–460nm) causes tissue to autofluoresce. Normal mucosa: bright apple-green fluorescence (from flavins and collagen cross-links). Abnormal/dysplastic/malignant mucosa: loss of fluorescence — appears dark or brown ("VEL-negative"). Sensitivity: high (~97%), Specificity: lower (~52%) → significant false positive rate (inflammation, melanin deposits, healing areas all cause false positives). Controversy: limited RCT evidence of benefit above COE alone. Role: Adjunct to COE, not standalone screening tool.

4

OralCDx Brush Biopsy (Transepithelial Biopsy)

Specially designed circular brush rotated firmly over the lesion to collect cells from the full thickness of the epithelium (not just surface cells like a regular smear). Computer-Assisted Analysis (AI-driven cytological analysis). Result: Negative (no atypical cells), Positive/Atypical (proceed to scalpel biopsy). Use: Triage tool for clinically equivocal lesions where conventional biopsy is deferred. Not a replacement for tissue biopsy. Not recommended for obviously malignant lesions (just proceed to incisional biopsy).

5

ViziLite Plus (Chemiluminescent Light)

Patient rinses with 1% acetic acid (removes glycoprotein layer), then examined with chemiluminescent light stick. Normal epithelium: absorbs light (appears dark). Abnormal epithelium: reflects light (appears white/bright = acetowhite). Similar controversy to VELscope — high false positive rate, limited evidence above COE.

6

Salivary Biomarkers (Research Stage — Future)

Saliva is a "liquid biopsy" for oral cancers. Biomarkers under investigation: Cytokines (IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α), miRNA profiles (miR-200a, miR-31), DNA methylation markers, proteomics (S100A8, S100A9), cell-free DNA analysis. Advantages: non-invasive, potentially high-throughput. Challenges: validation, standardisation, point-of-care implementation. Not yet validated for routine clinical use.

⚠️ Critical Principle

All adjunct screening tools are supplements — they do NOT replace tissue biopsy for definitive diagnosis. A positive screening test must always be followed by histopathological examination. A negative screening test does NOT exclude malignancy if there is clinical suspicion.

B. Radiation Therapy

📡 IMRT — Standard of Care
  • Intensity-Modulated Radiation Therapy (IMRT)
  • Spares parotid glands → reduces xerostomia vs 3D-CRT
  • Allows dose sculpting around critical structures (spinal cord, brain stem)
  • Dose — Primary RT: 66–70 Gy in 33–35 fractions (over 6–7 weeks)
  • Dose — Adjuvant RT: 60–66 Gy in 30–33 fractions
  • Starts 4–6 weeks post-surgery (wound healing)
⚠️ Indications for Adjuvant RT
  • Close surgical margins (1–5mm)
  • Pathological T3 or T4 disease
  • Multiple positive lymph nodes (≥2)
  • Perineural invasion (PNI)
  • Lymphovascular invasion (LVI)
  • Level IV or V lymph node involvement
  • Note: Positive margins + ENE → adjuvant chemoradiation (not RT alone)
🔴 When to Add Concurrent Chemotherapy to Adjuvant RT?

Only TWO features mandate adjuvant concurrent chemoradiation (per RTOG 9501 and EORTC 22931 trials):

  • 1. Positive surgical margins (R1)
  • 2. Pathological extranodal extension (pENE)

Drug of choice: Cisplatin 100mg/m² every 3 weeks during RT (3 cycles). These features confer the greatest benefit from adding chemotherapy.

RT Complications

⚡ Acute (During/Immediately After RT)
  • Mucositis (most troublesome) — grade 1–4; manage with mouthwash, analgesics, nutrition support
  • Xerostomia — dry mouth from parotid gland irradiation
  • Dysgeusia — altered/lost taste sensation
  • Dysphagia — swallowing difficulty
  • Radiation dermatitis — erythema to moist desquamation of skin
  • Fatigue, anorexia, weight loss
⏳ Late (Months to Years After RT)
  • Xerostomia — most common late effect (often permanent if parotid irradiated)
  • Trismus — masticatory muscle fibrosis (begin physio during/after RT!)
  • Osteoradionecrosis (ORN) — exposed necrotic bone (>3 months)
  • Radiation caries — cervical decay from xerostomia
  • Hypothyroidism — thyroid in RT field (monitor TFTs)
  • Fibrosis, lymphoedema, dysphagia
  • Rare: carotid artery disease, second malignancy
☢️ Osteoradionecrosis (ORN) — Key Complication
2–5% incidence after RT
  • Definition: Exposed, necrotic bone within the radiation field, persisting >3 months, WITHOUT tumour recurrence (distinguish from recurrence!)
  • Site: Mandible >>> Maxilla (mandible has end-arterial supply, more vulnerable)
  • Pathogenesis (Marx hypothesis): Hypovascular, hypoxic, hypocellular tissue → impaired wound healing → chronic necrosis
  • Risk factors: RT dose >60 Gy (mandible in field), dental extractions in irradiated field, poor oral hygiene, dental infection, trauma, alcohol, smoking
  • Stages (Marx classification): Stage I → responds to conservative Rx + HBO | Stage II → doesn't respond to HBO, surgery needed | Stage III → full-thickness necrosis, pathological fracture, orocutaneous fistula → resection + free flap
  • Treatment: Conservative (antibiotics, debridement, irrigation), Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy (HBO) — 30 sessions pre-op, 10 post-op (100% O2 at 2.4 atm), Surgical sequestrectomy or resection, Pentoxifylline + Tocopherol (vitamin E) = PENTOCLO protocol (anti-fibrotic effect)
  • Prevention: Pre-RT dental clearance ≥3 weeks before RT, fluoride trays, meticulous OH, avoid post-RT extractions (if unavoidable, use antibiotics + HBO cover)

C. Systemic Therapy

💉 Concurrent Chemotherapy
  • Cisplatin 100mg/m² every 3 weeks (×3 during RT) — STANDARD
  • Mechanism: DNA intrastrand crosslinks → radiosensitisation
  • Toxicities: Nephrotoxicity (adequate hydration!), ototoxicity, neurotoxicity, myelosuppression, nausea/vomiting
  • Alternatives: Carboplatin AUC5–6 (renally impaired), Cetuximab (cisplatin intolerant)
  • Induction TPF: Docetaxel + Cisplatin + 5-FU (before definitive Rx — controversial)
🎯 Cetuximab (Anti-EGFR Antibody)
  • Monoclonal antibody targeting EGFR (overexpressed in >90% of HNSCCs)
  • With RT: Alternative to cisplatin (Bonner trial — improved locoregional control)
  • With chemotherapy: For recurrent/metastatic HNSCC (EXTREME regimen)
  • Signature side effect: Acneiform papulopustular rash (severity correlates with treatment response — a good prognostic sign!)
  • Also: Hypomagnesaemia, infusion reactions (pre-medicate)
🛡️ Immunotherapy (Anti-PD-1 Checkpoint Inhibitors)
  • Pembrolizumab (Keytruda — anti-PD-1): First-line R/M HNSCC if PD-L1 CPS ≥1; KEYNOTE-048 trial
  • Nivolumab (Opdivo — anti-PD-1): Platinum-refractory R/M HNSCC; Checkmate 141 trial
  • PD-L1 testing: CPS (Combined Positive Score) — proportion of PD-L1 staining tumour cells + immune cells
  • CPS ≥1: Pembrolizumab monotherapy or + chemo | CPS <1: Pembrolizumab + chemo only
  • Response predictors: High CPS (≥20), high TMB (tumour mutational burden), HPV+, high TIL density
💊 Palliative Systemic Therapy
  • First-line R/M: Pembrolizumab ± Cisplatin + 5-FU (CPS ≥1 → mono if PS good)
  • EXTREME regimen: Cetuximab + Platinum + 5-FU (PD-L1 negative or pembrolizumab contraindicated)
  • Second-line: Docetaxel, Paclitaxel, Methotrexate (low dose)
  • Goal: Symptom control, quality of life, modest survival benefit

HPV Status and Prognosis

HPV+ Oropharyngeal SCC 🟢
  • 5-year survival: ~80–85%
  • Younger patients, non-smokers
  • Better locoregional control
  • Better response to RT and chemoRT
  • Lower risk of second primary
  • Active trials: de-escalation of treatment (less chemo, less RT dose)
HPV− Oropharyngeal SCC 🔴
  • 5-year survival: ~40–50%
  • Older patients, tobacco/alcohol users
  • Worse locoregional control
  • Higher risk of second primary (field cancerization)
  • Standard treatment intensity required
💡 HPV in Oral Cavity SCC

HPV+ status in oral cavity SCC (as opposed to oropharyngeal SCC) has less clearly established prognostic benefit. The dramatic HPV advantage applies primarily to oropharyngeal cancers (tonsil, base of tongue). For true oral cavity SCC, tobacco/alcohol risk factors remain dominant.

Prognostic Factors

🔴 Poor Prognostic Factors
  • Lymph node metastasis — single most important negative prognostic factor
  • Extranodal extension (ENE) — dramatically worsens prognosis
  • Advanced T stage (T3, T4)
  • Positive surgical margins
  • Deep invasion (DOI >10mm)
  • Poor histological grade (G3/G4)
  • Perineural invasion (PNI)
  • Lymphovascular invasion (LVI)
  • Multiple LN involvement
  • HPV negative (oropharynx)
  • Retromolar trigone location
  • Distant metastasis (M1)
  • Poor performance status
🟢 Good Prognostic Factors
  • Early T stage (T1, T2 with small DOI)
  • Node-negative neck (N0)
  • Clear surgical margins (>5mm)
  • Well-differentiated histology (G1)
  • No perineural invasion
  • No lymphovascular invasion
  • HPV positive (oropharynx)
  • Lip location
  • Small DOI (<5mm)
  • Good performance status
  • Young age
  • No tobacco/alcohol use

Patterns of Treatment Failure

Locoregional Recurrence

70% of failures. Most within 2 years of treatment. Presents as local recurrence at primary site or regional nodal recurrence. Salvage surgery possible in selected cases.

Distant Metastasis

15–20% of failures. Most common site: Lung (>60%), then Liver (~30%), Bone (~20%). Usually late presentation, indicates systemic disease.

Second Primary Tumour

3–7% per year risk. Field cancerization concept. Upper aerodigestive tract (larynx, oesophagus, lung — especially in smokers). Annual surveillance imaging essential.
